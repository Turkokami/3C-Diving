Cathodic protection is the least visible system on a vessel and one of the most consequential. It works silently, it costs very little relative to what it prevents, and its failure produces no alarm — just corrosion, somewhere expensive, discovered later.

This page covers how sacrificial protection actually works, how to tell when it is failing, why in-water renewal is worth doing rather than deferring to the next docking, and what a faster-than-expected consumption rate is telling you.

## How sacrificial protection works

Put two dissimilar metals in seawater and connect them electrically and you have made a battery. Seawater is the electrolyte, and current flows from the less noble metal to the more noble one. The less noble metal corrodes; the more noble one is protected.

Cathodic protection uses this deliberately. A sacrificial anode is a block of metal chosen to be **less noble than the structure you want to protect** — commonly zinc or aluminium alloy on a steel hull. Connected to the hull, it becomes the metal that corrodes, and the steel is protected for as long as the anode has material and surface area to give.

That last clause matters more than most operators realise. Protection is not a switch that is on until the anode vanishes. **As an anode is consumed its surface area falls, and with it the current it can deliver.** A heavily wasted anode is still visibly present while providing much less protection than its remaining bulk suggests. This is why the working practice is to renew at around half consumed rather than to run anodes to destruction.

### Where the demand is highest

Protection demand is not uniform across a hull. Three areas consistently consume fastest.

**The running gear.** A bronze propeller and a steel hull sitting in seawater is a strong galvanic couple, and the stern is consequently the most demanding area on most vessels. Shaft, rudder, bracket and boss anodes are usually the first to go. This is why anode condition is checked as a matter of course whenever a diver is at the stern for [propeller work](/services/propeller-services/).

**Anywhere coating has broken down.** Coating is the primary defence; cathodic protection handles what gets past it. Where coating has failed and bare steel is exposed, the demand on the anodes rises sharply. An unexpectedly high consumption rate is often the first evidence of coating breakdown somewhere.

**Sea chests and internal spaces.** Anodes inside sea chests and similar spaces are protecting areas that matter operationally and are almost never seen. They get overlooked precisely because they are out of sight. See [sea chest inspection and repair support](/services/sea-chest-services/).

## What failure actually costs

When protection lapses, corrosion does not stop. It relocates. The next least noble metal in the circuit takes over the sacrificial role, and that means whatever the anodes were protecting.

In practice: hull plate, particularly around the stern and any area of coating breakdown. Shaft surfaces, which are expensive and awkward to repair. Rudder stock areas and pintles. Fastenings and small components, which fail disproportionately because they have little material to lose. Sea chest internals, where wastage is discovered late and repaired at inconvenience.

The economics are stark and worth stating plainly. **Anodes are consumables measured in hundreds of dollars. What they protect is measured in tens of thousands, and mostly needs a drydock to repair.** Very little else in marine maintenance offers that ratio.

## Why in-water renewal is worth doing

The traditional pattern is to renew anodes at drydocking. On a five-year special survey cycle, that means protection is designed to last the interval, and if it does not, the vessel runs unprotected until the docking comes round.

That is a bad way to manage a system whose failure mode is silent and cumulative.

In-water renewal decouples anode replacement from the docking schedule. Protection is restored **when it is needed**, which is what the system requires to work as intended. And because anodes are checked and changed on a shorter cycle, the consumption data accumulates — which is what makes it possible to predict rather than react.

For vessels operating on this coast the case is stronger still, because warm Gulf water drives higher biological and electrochemical activity than colder trades. A protection scheme sized for a northern operating profile will not necessarily behave the same way here.

### The two attachment types

**Bolt-on anodes** are the straightforward case. Remove the wasted anode, clean the mounting face back to sound metal so the electrical connection is genuinely made, fit the new anode, secure it properly. The electrical continuity is the part that matters and the part most easily got wrong — an anode that is mechanically attached but not electrically connected is decoration.

**Welded anodes** require underwater welding, and that brings the weld class question with it. Anode attachment is normally not a primary structural weld, but the standard still has to be defined rather than assumed, and the surrounding plate condition still governs what is achievable. See [underwater welding and cutting support](/services/underwater-welding/).

In both cases growth has to come off the area first, which is one of several reasons anode work sits naturally alongside [hull cleaning](/services/hull-cleaning/).

## Reading the consumption rate

The single most useful thing an anode survey produces is not the replacement. It is the rate.

Record percentage consumed, by location, at each visit. Over two or three visits a pattern emerges, and that pattern is diagnostic:

**Steady, predictable consumption** across the expected areas means the system is working as designed. Renewal can be scheduled with confidence.

**A sudden increase** means something changed. Coating breakdown exposing more steel is the most common explanation. Stray current is the next — shore power, welding equipment working nearby, or a fault in an electrical system finding a path through the water. A new dissimilar metal introduced into the circuit during a repair will do it. So will a substantial change in operating profile, particularly more time sitting idle.

**Uneven consumption** — some anodes gone, others barely touched — usually points to a distribution or continuity problem. Either the protection is not reaching where it should, or some anodes are not properly connected and are contributing nothing.

**Anodes barely consumed at all** sounds like good news and sometimes is not. An anode that is not being consumed may not be electrically connected, in which case the area it was supposed to protect has been unprotected for however long that has been true.

That interpretation is why an anode survey is worth more than an anode count. A contractor who reports "twelve anodes, six replaced" has told you what they did. One who reports consumption by location against the previous visit has told you something about your vessel.

## Zinc or aluminium, and why it matters here

Anode alloy is not a detail. The two common sacrificial materials behave differently, and the difference is relevant on this coast specifically.

**Zinc** has been the traditional choice for seawater service and performs predictably in full-salinity, warm water. Its weakness is brackish and low-salinity conditions, where zinc anodes can passivate — developing a surface film that reduces their output substantially while leaving them looking perfectly serviceable. A passivated anode is arguably worse than a consumed one, because the consumed anode is obviously finished and the passivated one is not.

**Aluminium alloy** anodes generally offer more capacity per unit mass and continue working across a wider salinity range, which is why they have displaced zinc in much commercial service.

Why this matters at Brownsville: the salinity here is not constant. The channel takes freshwater down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande, and a vessel spending extended periods at a berth in this system sees a different environment from one in open Gulf water. For tonnage that sits — and this port has a great deal of it, given it captures over 85% of US Navy and MARAD ship recycling — that variation is part of the operating condition rather than an occasional excursion.

The practical point is not that one alloy is correct everywhere. It is that an anode scheme specified for a different trade may not perform as intended here, and that unexplained wastage behaviour is worth investigating rather than simply compensating for with more metal.

There is also a compatibility question. Mixing anode materials on the same structure, or introducing an anode alloy that does not suit the metals in the circuit, produces results nobody predicted. Where anodes have been replaced piecemeal over years by different parties, it is worth establishing what is actually fitted before adding more.

## What a useful anode report contains

An anode survey is worth what its record is worth, and most anode records are worth very little because they only say what was replaced.

A useful one identifies **every anode by position**, against the vessel's own references or a marked arrangement drawing, so the same anode can be found again next time. It records **percentage consumed for each**, which is the number the whole programme runs on. It notes **condition beyond consumption** — whether the anode is intact, whether the mounting is sound, and critically whether the electrical connection appears good, because an anode that is not connected is not protecting anything regardless of its size.

It records **what was replaced and what was left**, with the reason. It notes **the condition of the surrounding coating and plate**, because that context is what explains the consumption rate. And it states **what could not be assessed** — anodes obscured by growth that was not removed, positions that could not be accessed, or areas where visibility prevented a proper look.

Compared against the previous survey, that record turns into a consumption rate, and the rate is what allows the next replacement to be scheduled into a convenient berth window instead of discovered at an inconvenient one.

## Impressed current systems

Some vessels use impressed current cathodic protection rather than, or in addition to, sacrificial anodes — a powered system driving current through permanent anodes, regulated against reference cells.

The underwater components can be inspected in the water: anode condition, reference cell condition, the state of the dielectric shields around them, and any physical damage. Faults frequently announce themselves indirectly, as wastage patterns elsewhere on the hull that do not make sense, or as coating damage around the anodes where output has been excessive.

What an in-water inspection contributes is the physical condition of the underwater elements, which is the part the system's own instrumentation cannot see.

## Doing it at the Port of Brownsville

Local conditions bear on this work in two ways.

**Water chemistry and temperature.** The Gulf is warm, and warm seawater is a more active electrolyte than cold. Consumption rates here are generally higher than the same vessel would see in a northern trade, which matters when a protection scheme was sized elsewhere. Salinity variation is also real on this stretch — freshwater coming down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande alters the local picture, particularly for vessels spending long periods in one place.

**Idle time.** This port has substantial layberth activity, given it captures more than 85% of US Navy and MARAD ship-recycling business, with SteelCoast alone operating an 80-acre complex and roughly 4,000 ft of main-channel frontage. Vessels that sit for extended periods have a different protection profile from vessels that trade continuously, and inactive tonnage awaiting tow or scrapping is routinely running on anodes nobody has looked at in years — which matters if that vessel is going to be moved.

**Visibility.** The channel carries silt. For anode work this is a minor factor, since the work is close-in and largely tactile. It affects the photographic record of before-and-after condition more than it affects the job.

## Stray current, and why it is worth taking seriously

Of all the causes of unexplained anode consumption, stray current is the one most often missed, because it originates outside the vessel's own electrochemistry.

Stray current corrosion happens when current from some external source finds a path through the water and through the structure. It leaves the structure at some point to return to its source, and metal is lost where it leaves. The damage can be rapid, and it is often localised in a way that ordinary galvanic corrosion is not.

Common sources in a working port are shore power connections with a fault or a poor earth, welding equipment operating on an adjacent vessel or on the quay with its return path not properly established, and cathodic protection systems on neighbouring structures or vessels. A berth shared with construction activity is a more likely environment for this than a berth in isolation, which is worth bearing in mind given the scale of LNG and marine construction work currently under way at this port.

The signs are worth knowing. Anode consumption far outside the expected rate. Consumption concentrated in one area with no obvious galvanic explanation. Localised pitting or metal loss on the hull in a pattern that does not match the coating condition. Coating damage that looks like it was driven from the metal outward rather than degraded from the surface in.

An in-water survey cannot diagnose an electrical fault, but it can identify the pattern that says one probably exists — and that is usually the first anyone knows about it. Where we see it, we will say so, and the follow-up is electrical rather than diving.

## Safety on anode work

Anode replacement puts a diver in close, sustained contact with the hull, frequently at the stern near running gear and often adjacent to sea suctions and openings.

The controlling requirement is the same one that governs all in-water hull work: propulsion, thrusters, sea suctions and overboard discharges within the working area must be positively isolated, locked out and tagged before entry, with named accountability, confirmed physically rather than by radio. Where welding is involved, the electrical isolation and the control of the welding circuit are added to that.

Beyond the job-specific measures, commercial diving in the United States operates under 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, which sets minimum dive team composition, continuous tending, depth limits and decompression chamber provisions. Those requirements apply identically to every contractor, and a quote that only works with a smaller team has left out something the standard requires.

## Building an anode programme

The most valuable version of this service is not a one-off replacement. It is a record.

Start with a survey: every anode located, identified and assessed, with percentage consumed recorded against position. Renew what is past the threshold. Then repeat on an interval, and let the consumption rate set the interval rather than the calendar.

Within two or three cycles you have something genuinely useful — a vessel-specific consumption profile that predicts when protection will need renewing, flags when something has changed in the electrical picture, and lets anode work be planned into berth windows instead of discovered at a docking.

Tell us the vessel, its anode arrangement if you have the drawings, when the anodes were last renewed, and where it has been operating. If you are already booking hull or running-gear work, the anode survey should be on the same visit — the diver is on the hull anyway, and the marginal cost is small compared with what it prevents.
