The running gear is where a small defect produces a large bill. A propeller is a precision surface doing concentrated work, and anything that interferes with it — growth, roughness, a wrap, a damaged edge — costs fuel and speed immediately and can cost bearings, seals and shafts if it is left alone.

This page covers the three things that bring us to a stern: clearing something that has wrapped, polishing blades back toward their intended finish, and inspecting after something has gone wrong. They arrive by very different routes and on very different timescales.

## Clearing a wrapped propeller

This is the emergency version of the job, and it is common on this coast.

### What ends up around a shaft

**Rope and line.** Mooring line, lost line, line from other vessels' operations. The most frequent cause by a distance.

**Net and trawl gear.** With around 140 boats in the Brownsville–Port Isabel shrimp fleet — second in the nation by value and volume of wild-caught shrimp — there is a lot of net in the water, and not all of it is attached to a boat. Trawlers foul on their own gear, on lost gear, and on each other's.

**Wire and cable.** Less common, considerably worse. Wire does not part, it cuts, and it damages seals and shaft surfaces in the process.

**Plastic, tarpaulin, strapping, debris.** Especially after weather, and especially in a working port with substantial construction and recycling activity.

### Why it is not just an inconvenience

A wrap does three things at once. It **destroys efficiency**, because the blades are no longer moving clean water. It **loads the shaft asymmetrically**, which is what the vibration is telling you. And, worst, it **attacks the stern tube seal** — line under tension against a rotating shaft generates heat and abrasion exactly where the seal has to be intact. A seal failure turns a bad afternoon into a docking.

That is why running on to the next port with a known wrap is usually a false economy. The wrap costs fuel the whole way, and the risk it is running is the seal.

### What clearing actually involves

The diver works the wrap off with cutting tools, taking it in stages so material coming free does not trap or load anything unpredictably. Loose line is quick. Line that has melted under heat and welded itself into a hard mass on the shaft is slow and takes real cutting. Wire is slowest and needs the most care, because the objective is to remove it without adding to the damage it has already done to the shaft surface.

Once it is off, the important part begins: **checking what it did on the way in.** Seal area, shaft surface, rope guard, blade edges, and whether anything is deformed. A clearance job that ends the moment the last strand comes off has skipped the part that matters.

Isolation is absolute on this work. The shaft must not be able to turn. Propulsion is positively isolated, locked out and tagged, with names against it, before the diver enters the water — agreed with the vessel and confirmed physically, never over the radio.

## Polishing, and why the return is so high

Propeller polishing is the most under-used piece of vessel maintenance in this market, and the reason is that its value is not intuitive.

A hull is a large area doing simple work. A propeller is a small area doing precise work. Blade surfaces are shaped to generate thrust efficiently, and their performance is sensitive to roughness in a way flat plate is not. So a modest amount of fouling or a degraded surface finish on a blade costs a disproportionate amount of thrust — and thrust that is not generated has to be paid for in fuel.

The practical consequence: **on a hull maintenance visit, the propeller is usually the highest-return single item.** It is a small fraction of the work and a large fraction of the benefit. Yet it is routinely treated as an optional extra, or skipped because the hull looked like the priority.

### What polishing does and does not do

It restores surface finish and removes fouling and light deposits, returning the blade toward its design condition. It does **not** repair damage, correct deformation, rebalance a propeller, or fix cavitation erosion that has already removed material. Those are drydock matters.

Polishing works best as maintenance rather than rescue — done regularly, alongside [hull cleaning](/services/hull-cleaning/), so the surface never degrades far. A propeller polished on a sensible interval stays close to its design efficiency continuously.

## Inspection after a strike or a change in behaviour

The third route to the stern is diagnostic, and it is the one that should move fastest.

### Symptoms worth acting on

**Sudden vibration.** Something changed. A blade struck something, a section is missing or bent, something is wrapped, or alignment has been disturbed.

**A drop in speed or a rise in fuel at the same load.** Slower onset, usually fouling or surface degradation, sometimes damage that has not announced itself.

**A noise or a felt impact.** Somebody on board knows something happened. Take that seriously; it is better information than any instrument.

**Grounding or hard contact.** Even where the hull appears fine, the running gear is the lowest and most exposed part of the vessel and takes contact first.

### What gets examined

Blade condition across all faces, leading and trailing edges, tips, and any pattern of cavitation erosion — the pattern itself is diagnostic and tells you about the flow, not just the damage. Then the hub and fairwater, the shaft and its seal area, the rope guard, and the anodes protecting the running gear.

That last item is worth a note. Running-gear anodes are frequently the most consumed on a vessel because of the electrochemical environment around a bronze propeller and a steel hull. Checking them costs nothing once a diver is already at the stern, and finding them wasted is a cheap catch. See [anode replacement and cathodic protection](/services/anode-replacement/).

### The decision the inspection produces

The point of a damage inspection is not a description. It is a decision:

- **Trade on normally** — no significant damage found.
- **Trade on with a limit** — damage that is stable and tolerable at reduced revolutions or speed, with a docking planned.
- **Stop** — damage where continuing risks turning a blade problem into a shaft, bearing or seal problem.

Getting to that decision requires documentation good enough to support it, particularly where insurers or class are involved. Location referenced to the vessel's own identifiers, imagery with scale and context, and an explicit statement of what was and was not examined. See [underwater inspection](/services/underwater-inspection/) for how that record is built.

## Cavitation, and reading what the blade is telling you

Cavitation erosion is worth understanding because the damage is a message about the flow, not just a defect to be measured.

When local pressure over a blade section drops below the vapour pressure of the water, vapour cavities form and then collapse violently as they move into higher pressure. Those collapses happen against the blade surface, and repeated over time they remove metal. The result is a characteristic pitted, spongy erosion rather than the clean deformation of an impact.

**Where the erosion sits tells you why it is happening.** Damage concentrated near the leading edge on the suction face suggests one flow condition; damage further aft or at the tips suggests another; erosion on the pressure face points somewhere different again. A pattern that has appeared where there was none before means something upstream changed — a damaged appendage disturbing the inflow, a fouled hull altering the wake, an alteration in how the vessel is being operated, or accumulated damage to the blade itself.

This matters commercially because polishing an eroded blade treats the surface and not the cause. If the flow condition producing the cavitation is still there, the erosion returns. Recording the pattern, with imagery and location, is what lets whoever is responsible for the machinery make that call.

The same logic applies to a hull. A propeller works in the wake the hull delivers to it, so a heavily fouled hull changes what the propeller is being asked to do. That is one of the reasons running gear and [hull cleaning](/services/hull-cleaning/) belong on the same visit rather than being scheduled independently.

## What "back to design finish" actually means

Polishing is often sold as though it were a single operation with a single outcome. It is not, and being precise about it prevents disappointment.

A propeller leaves the manufacturer with a specified surface finish. In service that finish degrades through fouling, corrosion, cavitation erosion, and general roughening. Polishing addresses the first and to a degree the second. It restores the surface as far as the remaining material allows.

What it cannot do is put back metal that has gone. A blade that has lost material to erosion has a different geometry than it left with, and no amount of surface work restores that. Nor can in-water polishing correct a blade that is bent, cracked, or has lost a section: those are geometry and balance problems that need the propeller worked properly, which means a dock.

So the honest framing is that polishing returns the propeller toward its best achievable current condition, and that regular polishing keeps "best achievable current condition" much closer to the original than intermittent polishing does. A propeller attended to on every hull visit stays close to design. One polished every few years spends most of its life well below it.

## Running gear on the fishing fleet

Trawler work deserves its own note because the economics are completely different from commercial tonnage.

A shrimp boat that is tied up is not earning, and the margins in that fishery are thin — the Brownsville–Port Isabel fleet has contracted from roughly 500 boats in the mid-1980s to around 140 today, under pressure from deckhand shortages, fuel cost and imported farmed shrimp. A wrap that keeps a boat at the dock for two days is a serious matter for that operator in a way a comparable delay is not for a large commercial vessel.

The work itself also differs. Trawlers foul on their own gear and on lost gear, so the material is usually net and heavy line rather than mooring rope. Vessels are smaller, shafts are more accessible, and turnaround expectations are measured in hours. Anodes and running gear on working boats are frequently overdue simply because there has been no reason to look.

The practical approach is to do the clearance, then take five minutes on the rest of the running gear while already there — blade edges, shaft, seal area, rope guard, anodes. It costs almost nothing at that point and it is where the next problem is usually visible.

## Working conditions on this coast

Two local factors matter for running-gear work.

**Visibility.** The Brownsville Ship Channel carries silt, and rainfall through the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande reduces clarity further. For clearing and polishing this is largely a non-issue — the work is close-in and much of it is done by feel. It matters for the documentation side: recording blade damage convincingly is harder in poor water, which is worth knowing if the imagery is going to an insurer.

**The two fleets.** The port moved 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years, alongside a substantial fishing fleet. The commercial side wants planned polishing on a schedule that fits a berth window. The fishing side wants a wrap cleared today because the boat is not earning while it is tied up. Those are different jobs with different urgency, and a contractor working this coast needs to be set up for both.

## Anodes on the running gear

One item deserves calling out separately because it is the most consistently overlooked thing at the stern.

The running gear is the most electrochemically demanding area on most vessels. A bronze propeller and a steel hull sitting in seawater form a galvanic couple, and the sacrificial anodes protecting that area are consumed faster than anodes elsewhere on the hull. Shaft anodes, rudder anodes and any anodes on the boss or bracket are routinely the first to disappear.

The consequence of letting them go is not abstract. Once the protection is gone, the corrosion attacks the least noble metal in the circuit — which can mean shaft surfaces, the boss, the rudder stock area or fastenings. That is expensive damage in a place that is awkward to repair.

Because a diver is already at the stern for any clearing, polishing or inspection job, checking anode consumption there costs essentially nothing. Recording the percentage consumed on each visit builds a consumption rate for that specific vessel, and a consumption rate is what lets replacement be scheduled rather than discovered. See [anode replacement and cathodic protection](/services/anode-replacement/) for how that fits into a wider protection scheme.

## Safety on running-gear work

Every propeller job shares one controlling hazard: the diver is working on the machine that moves the vessel.

**Positive isolation of propulsion and thrusters, locked out and tagged, is non-negotiable** and is confirmed physically before entry. Where a vessel has controllable-pitch machinery, hydraulics associated with pitch control are part of that isolation, not an afterthought. Where shaft rotation can be induced by water flow, the shaft is secured against turning.

Beyond that, commercial diving in the United States operates under 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, which sets the minimum team, continuous tending of the diver, depth limits and chamber requirements. These apply to every contractor equally. Ask for the written dive plan, the supervisor's qualifications, the isolation arrangements and who is signing for them, and the emergency plan for that berth.

## Planning running-gear work into a berth window

Running-gear jobs are unusually well suited to being fitted around commercial operations, provided one thing is arranged in advance.

The work is concentrated at the stern, which is normally the quiet end of a vessel during cargo operations. The diver needs a small area of water, access from the quay or a workboat, and a defined period during which the machinery cannot move. That is a much lighter footprint than a full hull job.

The constraint is the isolation, and it is worth understanding what it involves so the request does not arrive as a surprise. Propulsion has to be positively isolated and locked out. On a vessel with controllable-pitch machinery, the hydraulics associated with pitch control are part of that, because a blade that can move is a hazard even if the shaft cannot turn. Thrusters near the working area come into it. And where flow past the vessel could induce the shaft to rotate, the shaft itself is physically secured.

Arranged in advance with the chief engineer, that is routine and takes very little time. Requested on arrival, it can hold a crew on the quay for hours while somebody is found who can authorise it. The single most useful thing an operator can do to make running-gear work efficient is to raise the isolation with the vessel at the point of booking rather than at the point of mobilisation.
