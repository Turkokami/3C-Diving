A fouled hull is a fuel bill that never stops running. Every day the vessel trades with growth on it, the engine is pushing that growth through the water, and the penalty is paid in fuel that produces no revenue. Cleaning it is one of the few pieces of vessel maintenance where the commercial case is arithmetic rather than judgement.

This page covers what fouling actually costs, how the work is done, why the rules on in-water cleaning have tightened, and why grooming a hull frequently is usually a better strategy than cleaning it heavily once a year. It also covers when in-water cleaning is the wrong answer.

## What fouling does to a vessel

Fouling is a sequence, not an event, and knowing where a hull is in that sequence determines what should be done about it.

**Conditioning film and slime.** Within hours of immersion a hull acquires an organic film, then a bacterial slime layer. This is the stage most operators ignore because the hull still looks clean from the deck. It is not cost-free: slime increases roughness and roughness increases frictional resistance, which is the largest component of resistance for most commercial hull forms at service speed.

**Soft growth.** Algae and weed establish on the slime layer. Now the effect is visible at the boot top and the penalty is unmistakable in the fuel figures.

**Hard growth — macrofouling.** Barnacles, tubeworms, mussels and the rest. This is a different order of problem. Hard growth is firmly attached, physically rough, and removing it takes real force — which is where the risk to the coating begins, and where the regulatory position changes.

The Gulf accelerates all of this. Warm, biologically productive water with a long growing season means a hull working the South Texas coast fouls faster than the same hull in a northern trade. Idle time makes it worse: a vessel sitting at a layberth is a stationary substrate, and this port has a great deal of layberth activity given it captures more than 85% of US Navy and MARAD ship-recycling business.

### Why the fuel penalty compounds

The important commercial point is the shape of the cost. Cleaning is a single line item on a single day. The fuel penalty is a percentage applied to every day of trading until the hull is cleaned.

That is why the arithmetic almost always favours acting sooner. A modest penalty running for eight months costs more than a larger cleaning bill paid at month two. Operators who track hull performance against a clean-hull baseline see this immediately; operators who clean "when it looks bad" are usually paying for months of drag they never measured.

## The rules have tightened, and that changes the strategy

In-water cleaning is regulated as an **underwater ship husbandry discharge** — material released during inspection, maintenance, cleaning and repair of hulls and appendages carried out while the vessel is waterborne. That is precisely what this work is.

The position now, under the EPA regime replacing the Vessel General Permit:

- **Discharge from in-water cleaning of macrofouling is prohibited without capture.**
- **Microfouling** cleaning of hulls and niche areas carries its own discharge requirements.
- **Major cleaning** is expected to take place in drydock or at a land-based facility where waste can be properly managed.
- **TBT-based coatings are prohibited**; a hull still carrying TBT requires a certified effective sealing coat.

Read those together and a strategy falls out of them. **The regulatory environment rewards never letting a hull reach macrofouling in the first place.** A hull groomed frequently enough to stay at the slime and light-soft-growth stage is cheaper to maintain, easier on its coating, better performing every day, and stays clear of the capture requirement that heavy in-water cleaning now attracts.

That is not a sales argument dressed as compliance. It is the same conclusion from three directions — fuel, coating life and regulation — which is unusual and worth acting on.

## Cleaning versus grooming

The distinction matters enough to be explicit about.

**Cleaning** is remedial. Fouling has established, and it is removed. The tooling is more aggressive because it has to be, the coating takes more of a beating, and the discharge question is live because there is real biomass coming off.

**Grooming** is preventive. Frequent, gentle passes that disturb the film before anything establishes on it. Less aggressive on the coating, quicker and cheaper per visit, keeps the vessel near its clean-hull performance continuously rather than sawtoothing between cleans, and produces far less material.

Grooming suits vessels with a predictable pattern — regular port calls, a fixed trade, or an operating base on this coast. It suits the port's tug, barge, workboat and offshore support traffic particularly well, because they are here often enough for short frequent visits to be practical.

Heavy cleaning still has a place: vessels arriving fouled from elsewhere, tonnage that has been idle, and hulls where nobody has been tracking condition. The point is that it should be the entry into a programme rather than the programme itself.

## Method, and why it is chosen after looking

There is no single correct tool. Method is matched to three things, and none of them can be assessed from an office.

**How heavy the growth is.** Slime and light soft growth need very little. Established hard growth needs real mechanical work.

**What the coating is and how old it is.** Coating systems differ substantially in how much abrasion they tolerate. Some are formulated to be groomed; others degrade quickly under repeated aggressive contact. An older system near the end of its life has less to give than a fresh one.

**What the surface is.** The flat of bottom, the vertical sides, the boot top, running gear and niche areas are not the same problem and are not treated identically.

The failure to avoid is taking off more coating than growth. Antifouling is a consumable that works by controlled release, and over-aggressive cleaning strips the working surface, shortens the system's life and brings the next docking forward. A cleaning job that saves fuel this quarter and costs a coating renewal next year has not saved anything.

This is why the first visit to an unfamiliar hull normally includes an [underwater inspection](/services/underwater-inspection/) element. Establishing coating type, coating condition, growth level and any existing damage is what makes the method choice defensible rather than a guess.

## Niche areas are where the operational problems live

The flat of bottom gets the attention because it is the biggest area and the fuel argument is about drag. But the places that generate the phone calls are smaller.

**Sea chests and gratings.** Fouling here restricts cooling water. That is not an efficiency problem, it is a machinery problem, and it announces itself as rising temperatures at the worst possible moment. See [sea chest inspection and repair support](/services/sea-chest-services/).

**Thruster tunnels.** Growth in a tunnel affects thrust and can affect the unit itself. Tunnels are also awkward, sheltered and consequently foul well.

**Running gear.** Growth on a propeller degrades efficiency out of proportion to its area, because the blade surface is doing precision work. [Propeller polishing](/services/propeller-services/) is often the highest-return single item on a hull maintenance visit.

**Rudder, stern gear, bilge keels, appendages and the boot top.** Sheltered, complex geometry, easily skipped, and reliably fouled.

A hull clean that covers the flat of bottom and skips the niches leaves most of the operational risk in place. If a quote does not say what it includes, ask.

## How this works at the Port of Brownsville

Three local factors shape the job here.

**Water clarity.** The channel carries silt, and rainfall through the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande drops visibility further. For cleaning this matters far less than for survey work, because much of the work is done close in and by feel. It does affect verification — establishing what the hull looked like before and after is harder in poor water, and worth planning around if the record matters to you.

**Berth availability and isolation.** The governing constraint is rarely the cleaning. It is getting the isolation agreed and confirmed, and holding a window in which propulsion, thrusters and sea suctions can stay shut down. That is a terminal and vessel conversation as much as a diving one.

**The traffic mix.** The port moved 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years, and it is the only deep-water port directly on the US–Mexico border. Alongside deep-draft commercial trade there is a roughly 140-boat shrimp fleet — second in the nation by value and volume of wild-caught shrimp — plus tug, barge and offshore support traffic, and substantial LNG construction activity as Rio Grande LNG builds toward 48 MTPA and Texas LNG progresses a second terminal.

Those fleets have genuinely different cleaning needs. A deep-draft vessel on a fixed rotation is a grooming candidate. A trawler that has been working is usually a running-gear job with a hull clean attached. A construction barge that has sat on station for months is a heavy clean.

## Measuring whether it worked

Hull cleaning is unusual among marine maintenance in that its benefit is directly measurable, and yet it is very rarely measured. Operators who do measure it stop treating cleaning as a cost and start treating it as an investment with a payback period, because that is what the numbers show it to be.

The method does not need to be sophisticated. What is required is a **baseline** — fuel consumption or shaft power at a known speed, draught and loading condition, ideally recorded when the hull was known to be clean. Performance is then tracked against that baseline over time, with allowance made for the obvious confounders: weather, sea state, current, draught and displacement, and hull trim.

What emerges is a curve. Performance degrades from the clean-hull baseline at a rate specific to that vessel and its operating pattern, and the shape of that curve tells you two commercially important things. It tells you **what fouling is currently costing** in fuel per day, which turns an abstract concern into a number. And it tells you **when the accumulated penalty exceeds the cost of cleaning**, which is the point at which cleaning pays for itself immediately.

For vessels operating on a regular local pattern, a few visits' worth of data is enough to establish an interval empirically rather than by rule of thumb. That is a considerably better basis than "annually" or "when it looks bad", both of which are near-certain to be wrong in one direction or the other.

If you already collect noon-report or performance-monitoring data, send it with the enquiry. It is more useful for planning the work than any description of the hull, and it lets the interval conversation start from evidence.

## When in-water cleaning is the wrong answer

Three situations where we will say so.

**The growth is heavy enough that it is a drydock job.** Extensive macrofouling means significant biomass, a capture requirement, real coating risk, and a long, expensive in-water operation that still may not produce a good result. If a docking is within reach, that is where it belongs.

**The coating has failed.** Cleaning a hull whose antifouling has broken down treats the symptom. The growth will return quickly because the thing that was supposed to prevent it has stopped working. The finding matters more than the clean.

**A survey is imminent and cleaning would compromise it.** Cleaning immediately before a class in-water survey changes what the surveyor can establish about coating condition, so it has to be discussed with them rather than assumed. See [UWILD and in-water survey support](/services/uwild-in-water-survey/).

## Invasive species and why capture is being taken seriously

The discharge rules around in-water cleaning are not only about water quality. A significant part of the concern is biological: a hull is a vector, and cleaning one in the water releases whatever it has been carrying into that port.

Fouling organisms picked up in one region travel attached to a hull and arrive somewhere they did not evolve. Where conditions suit them, they establish, and the consequences run from fouled intakes and infrastructure through to real ecological damage. That is the reasoning behind requiring capture for macrofouling removal — the material coming off a heavily fouled hull is exactly the material with the greatest potential to establish.

For an operator, the practical consequences are the same conclusion reached from the other direction: **a hull that never carries heavy growth is not carrying much of a biological cargo either.** Frequent grooming is both the cheaper maintenance strategy and the lower-risk one from a compliance and reputational point of view.

It is also worth knowing that this is an area where requirements are tightening rather than relaxing, and where individual ports and states can be stricter than the federal baseline. Confirming the position for the specific port before booking heavy in-water cleaning is sensible, and is something we will raise rather than leave to you to discover.

## Safety and isolation on a cleaning job

Cleaning puts a diver in prolonged close contact with the hull, frequently near openings and running gear, often for hours. Two things follow.

**Isolation is the controlling safety measure**, not a formality. Propulsion, thrusters, sea suctions and overboard discharges must be positively isolated, locked out and tagged, with names against it, before anyone enters the water. A sea suction starting while a diver is working a grating is the accident this industry exists to prevent. That agreement is made with the vessel and the terminal before mobilisation and confirmed physically at the berth.

**The diving standard is federal and applies to everyone.** Commercial diving in the United States operates under 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, which sets minimum dive team composition, tending, depth limits and chamber requirements. A cleaning price that only works with two people on site has left out a required role. Ask any contractor for the written dive plan for the specific job, the supervisor's qualifications, and the emergency arrangements for that berth.

## Setting up a maintenance interval

The most valuable outcome of a first visit is usually not the clean. It is the interval.

Once we have seen the hull, the coating and how the vessel operates, it is possible to say roughly how quickly this particular vessel fouls — and therefore how often it needs attention to stay near its clean-hull performance rather than sawtoothing. That converts an unpredictable annual expense into a planned, smaller, more frequent one, and it usually reduces total cost while improving performance every day in between.

Tell us the vessel, where it trades, how much time it spends idle, what coating it carries and when it was last docked. If you have fuel consumption data against a baseline, that is the most useful thing you can send. We will come back with what we would do on the first visit, what we would want to establish while we are down there, and what interval we would suggest once we have seen it.
