Objects end up on the bottom of a working port constantly. Some matter because they are valuable, some because they are needed, and some because they are now in the way of a vessel that has to come alongside. All three end up being the same technical problem: find it, get to it, and bring it up.

This page covers how searching is actually done in water you cannot see through, what determines whether a recovery is straightforward or difficult, and what to do in the first few minutes after something goes over the side.

## The first few minutes matter more than anything else

The single biggest factor in the cost of a recovery is the quality of the position, and position degrades fast.

**Mark it immediately** if it can be done safely. A buoy, a weighted line, anything that stays where the object went in. This is worth more than any amount of subsequent description.

**Take a GPS fix** on the spot rather than on where the vessel happens to be afterwards.

**Note the time and the state of the tide.** If the object is capable of moving — anything light, anything with surface area — a running tide will have carried it, and knowing which way and for how long turns a large search area into a small one.

**Write down what happened** while people remember. Which side, roughly how far out, whether it went straight down or was carried, and whether anything was seen after it entered.

**Establish what it is.** Dimensions, weight, material, and whether it has anything on it that would serve as a lifting point.

A precise position turns a search into a recovery. A vague one turns a recovery into a search, and searching is what costs money.

## Searching water you cannot see through

The Brownsville Ship Channel carries silt, and freshwater running down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande after rain reduces visibility further. Most recovery work here is done in conditions where a diver cannot see a useful distance. This is normal and it is worked around by method.

The principle is straightforward: **if you cannot see, you cover ground systematically instead.** A search is anchored to a datum — a fixed point established on the bottom — and worked outward in a pattern that guarantees coverage.

**Circular search.** A line from a fixed datum, worked in arcs, extending the radius each pass. Efficient where the position is reasonably good and the bottom is fairly clear.

**Jackstay search.** A line laid on the bottom between two points, with the diver working along it and the line moved across after each pass. Effective for covering a rectangular area methodically and well suited to searching along a berth face.

**Snag line.** A weighted line dragged between two points to catch on objects standing proud. Useful for a first pass over a large area, and for locating something substantial before committing to a detailed search.

What all of these have in common is that **coverage is recorded rather than assumed.** The value of a systematic search is not only that it finds things; it is that where it does not find something, you know with confidence that the object is not in the area covered. An unsystematic search that finds nothing tells you nothing at all.

That distinction is worth paying for. It is the difference between "we looked and did not find it" and "it is not in this area, so it is somewhere else, and here is where to look next."

## What typically ends up on the bottom

In a commercial port with the activity level of Brownsville — 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years — there is a broad range.

**Tools and equipment** over the side during work. The most common by number and usually the simplest to recover once located.

**Machinery and components**, dropped during lifts, transfers or repairs.

**Cargo**, from a container to a single item, with everything that implies for insurance and for the environment depending on what it is.

**Ground tackle.** Anchors, chain and wire, often lost under load and often partly buried.

**Construction plant and materials.** With Rio Grande LNG building toward 48 MTPA including two ship berths and a turning basin, and Texas LNG progressing a second terminal, there is substantial marine construction activity — and marine construction reliably puts material in the water. That work overlaps with [marine construction dive support](/services/marine-construction-support/).

**Fishing gear.** With around 140 boats in the Brownsville–Port Isabel fleet, there is a great deal of net and line in this water, some of it lost. It causes its own problems, notably [wrapped propellers](/services/propeller-services/).

**Vehicles, small craft and larger items**, which shade into [marine salvage](/services/marine-salvage/) as scale increases.

## Why the position you give governs the price

It is worth being explicit about the economics, because owners are often surprised by how much two apparently similar jobs differ.

A search costs roughly in proportion to the **area** that has to be covered, not to the value or size of the object. Covering ground systematically in poor visibility takes a predictable amount of diver time per square metre, and that time is the job.

The consequence is that the area is set almost entirely by the quality of the position. A marked spot, or a good GPS fix taken at the moment of loss, defines a small circle. A berth and a rough distance defines a larger one. "Somewhere along the quay while we were working that afternoon" defines an area that may be an order of magnitude bigger — and it will cost accordingly, because the search still has to be systematic to mean anything.

Two other factors move it. **Bottom conditions**: a clear, firm bottom is faster to cover than one littered with debris, where every contact has to be identified before moving on. And **whether the object could have moved**: something light, something with surface area, or something lost on a running tide may not be where it entered, which widens the area in a predictable direction if the tide state was noted and unpredictably if it was not.

None of that is a reason to avoid calling when the position is poor. It is a reason to spend the first five minutes after a loss getting the position as good as it can be — that five minutes is the cheapest part of the entire operation, and it has more effect on the total than anything we do afterwards.

## What we hand back

A recovery job produces more than the object, and the additional part is worth asking for.

**Where it was actually found**, against a fixed reference, which is frequently not where it was thought to be — useful information if the same operation is going to repeat.

**Its condition as found**, before recovery. If the item is going to be repaired, reused, claimed on insurance or examined, the state it was in on the bottom matters, and it is only recorded if somebody records it.

**What area was searched and confirmed clear.** This is the part with lasting value. Where an obstruction was the concern, a documented clearance over a defined area is what lets a terminal put a vessel back on that berth with confidence.

**What was found that was not being looked for.** Systematic searches in working ports routinely turn up other things on the bottom. Whoever owns the water usually wants to know.

## What makes a recovery difficult

Once an object is found, four things determine how hard it is to bring up.

**Weight, including what has been added.** An object on the bottom weighs what it weighs, less buoyancy, plus whatever it has filled with. A container that has flooded, a machine full of water, a vessel with silt in it — all considerably heavier than the dry item.

**Bottom suction.** This is the one that catches people out. An object settled into soft silt is held by suction that can substantially exceed its own weight, and it releases suddenly when it does release. Both facts have to be in the lift plan: the capacity has to allow for it, and the sudden release has to be anticipated so nothing is in the way when it happens.

**Rigging points.** Something has to take the load. Purpose-built lifting points are ideal and frequently absent. Improvised points have to be assessed for whether they will actually hold, because a rigging point that fails mid-lift drops the object, potentially onto a diver, and usually into a worse position than it started in.

**Access and orientation.** Whether rigging can be passed where it needs to go. Passing a strop beneath an object that has settled into the bottom is among the more demanding tasks in this work, particularly by feel.

## Recovery methods

**Direct lift by hand or by line** for small items. Straightforward once located.

**Lift bags.** Controlled buoyancy attached to the object. Well suited to moderate weights and to situations where crane access is poor. Requires care: an over-inflated bag produces an uncontrolled ascent, which is dangerous to anyone above and frequently damages the object.

**Crane or barge lift.** For heavier items, with the underwater side providing rigging and guidance. This becomes a coordinated lifting operation with everything that implies for exclusion zones and communication.

**Cut and recover in sections.** Where the object cannot be recovered whole. See [underwater cutting](/services/underwater-welding/).

**Leave it, deliberately.** Sometimes the right answer, provided it is a decision rather than an omission — and provided whoever owns the water agrees. Which brings us to the part owners often overlook.

## Obstructions are somebody else's problem too

An object on the bottom at a working berth is not only a loss to its owner. It is a hazard to the next vessel that comes alongside, and the terminal has a legitimate interest in it.

That changes the economics. A dropped item might not be worth recovering on its own value, but if it is sitting where a vessel needs to be, recovery is not really optional. The same applies to anything in or near the navigable channel, where the interested parties extend to the port authority and potentially the Coast Guard.

The practical advice is to tell the terminal early rather than hoping. A known object that is being dealt with is a manageable situation. An unknown object discovered by a vessel's hull is a considerably worse one, and the consequences of not having mentioned it are worse still.

**Confirming the bottom is clear afterwards** is part of the job for the same reason. A recovery that brings up the object but leaves parts of it behind has not finished, and the record should say what was confirmed clear and over what area.

## Sensitive recoveries

Some recoveries are not commercial. Evidence, personal effects, and searches following an incident in which somebody has been hurt or lost all fall into this category.

These are handled quietly and in coordination with whoever is leading — law enforcement, the Coast Guard, or the family's representatives. The requirements are different: chain of custody may matter, position and condition as found may matter more than speed, and the manner in which it is done matters to people who are having the worst day of their lives.

We do not publicise this work, we do not discuss it afterwards, and we do not use it as marketing. If you are dealing with something of this nature, call and say so, and it will be treated accordingly.

## Working with divers versus other methods

Diving is not the only way to find something on the bottom, and it is worth knowing where it fits.

**Sonar and acoustic methods** cover large areas quickly and work regardless of visibility. They are the right tool for a big search area or for locating something substantial, and they are commonly used to narrow a search before divers go in. What they do not do well is small objects, objects close to or under structure, objects among debris, or objects that have settled into soft bottom — all of which are common in a berth environment.

**Magnetometers** find ferrous material and can be effective where the object is steel and the bottom is not. In a working port with a steel-plated quay face, sheet piling, and decades of accumulated ferrous debris, the signal-to-noise problem is considerable.

**Remotely operated vehicles** are useful where visibility permits and where the task is looking rather than handling. In the silt-laden water typical of this channel their advantage over a diver narrows sharply, and they cannot pass a strop under an object settled into the bottom.

**Divers** are slower per unit area and are the only option that can identify by touch, work among and under structure, free a buried object, and rig it properly for lifting in one operation.

The practical answer on most jobs here is that the choice is not diving versus something else — it is what sequence uses each appropriately. Where an area is large and a position is poor, narrowing it acoustically first and then putting divers on the likely spots is usually faster and cheaper than either alone. Where the area is already small, or the object is under a berth or among debris, divers are the sensible starting point.

## Safety in search and recovery

The hazards here differ from routine hull work in one important respect: **the diver frequently does not know what is around them.**

**Entrapment** is the primary concern. Working by feel among unknown objects, debris, lost fishing gear and wreckage, in water where a problem cannot be seen developing. Search technique, line management and tending discipline are the controls.

**Unknown objects** may be unstable, sharp, under load, or hazardous in ways that are not apparent by touch. Anything not identified is treated as potentially all of those.

**Lifting operations** put loads over and around a diver. Exclusion, sequencing and clear communication between the water and the surface are what keep that safe, and nothing moves while somebody is in a position to be caught by it.

**Sudden release** — of suction, of a snagged line, of stored tension in rigging — is a real and underestimated hazard, and it is planned for rather than reacted to.

Commercial diving in the United States operates under 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, which sets the minimum dive team, continuous tending of the diver, depth limits and decompression provisions that apply to every contractor equally. In search work the tending discipline matters more than usual, because the tender's line is frequently the diver's only reference in water where nothing can be seen.

## Getting a search started

Call with the best position you have, what the object is, when it went in, and whether it is an obstruction as well as a loss. If you have marked the spot, say so — that single fact can change the scale of the job substantially.

We will tell you honestly what the search is likely to involve given the position quality, what we would do first, and where the cost is going to sit. Where the sensible answer is that the search area is too large to be economic against the object's value, we will say that too — and if it is an obstruction rather than a loss, we will say why that changes the answer.
