Every owner reaches the same point in the survey cycle: a docking is due, the yard slot is expensive, the voyage there and back is dead time, and somebody asks whether the vessel could be surveyed in the water instead. That question is what UWILD answers.

This page sets out what an in-water survey actually involves, who decides whether it is acceptable, what routinely stops one on this coast, and how the job is planned so that it does not get abandoned halfway. It is deliberately blunt about the constraints, because a UWILD that fails partway through costs an owner more than not attempting one.

## What UWILD is, and what it is not

**Underwater Inspection In Lieu of Drydocking.** The vessel stays afloat. A diver examines the underwater body while a class surveyor observes, and the society credits that examination against a docking survey it would otherwise require.

The phrase "in lieu" is the whole point and it is worth being precise about it. Class rules generally require two bottom examinations in each five-year special survey period, with a defined interval between them. A UWILD substitutes for one of those. It is not an additional inspection bolted onto the schedule, and it is not a commercial condition survey with a surveyor watching.

That distinction matters because it changes who sets the scope. On an owner's [condition inspection](/services/underwater-inspection/), you decide what gets looked at. On a UWILD, the class society decides — and the contractor's job is to deliver an examination that satisfies somebody else's written requirements.

### Who actually decides

Not us. Your class society decides three things, and none of them is negotiable at the berth:

- **Whether the vessel is eligible at all**, based on age, type, service, coating condition and survey history
- **What the examination must cover**, which for an ABS in-water survey is the entire vessel below the waterline
- **What evidence is acceptable**, including how the dive is observed and recorded

The practical consequence is that the first phone call should be to your society or your surveyor, not to a diving contractor. A contractor who tells you they can "get you a UWILD" without reference to your society is selling something they do not control.

## What an ABS in-water survey requires

ABS publishes its requirements, and they are specific enough to plan against. Four things stand out because each one is a place a job can fail.

**An approved service supplier.** The examination must be carried out by a qualified diver employed by a firm that ABS has approved as a service supplier. This is a credential held by the company, not a skill claimed by an individual, and it is verifiable. It is also the single most important question an owner can ask a bidder.

**Closed-circuit television with two-way communication.** The surveyor has to be able to see what the diver sees and speak to the diver while they are seeing it. This is not a camera strapped to a helmet recording for later review — it is a live link, so the surveyor can direct the examination, ask for a second look, and satisfy themselves on a point before the diver moves on.

**Surveyor attendance or documentation, according to vessel age and type.** The progress of the dive is monitored by the attending surveyor, and/or photographically documented, depending on the vessel. Older tonnage generally attracts closer attention.

**Adequate water clarity.** The water must be clear enough for the diver or camera to view the full height of the propeller and rudder in a single frame. ABS also expects the survey to be conducted somewhere reasonably protected, with calm, clear water. In practice the working benchmark is visibility better than about two metres.

> **Ask this before you sign anything.** Which society, which survey, and does the contractor hold that society's service supplier approval for it? A contractor who answers vaguely is a contractor whose survey may not be creditable — and you will discover that after the dive, not before.

Other societies — Lloyd's Register, DNV, Bureau Veritas, ClassNK and the rest — operate their own approval schemes and their own detailed requirements. They are broadly comparable in intent and differ in detail. Do not assume that approval with one transfers to another.

## Why visibility is the governing constraint here

Of everything on this page, this is the part specific to Brownsville, and it is the reason a local contractor is worth more than a cheaper one from up the coast.

The single-frame propeller and rudder criterion is a **visibility** criterion. It is not a matter of the diver being careful or the camera being good. Either the water lets you frame that shot or it does not.

The Brownsville Ship Channel is working water. It carries silt, it takes freshwater and sediment down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande after rain, it sees constant deep-draft traffic, and it has had substantial dredging activity associated with the channel deepening completed in June 2026 — which took the main channel from 42 ft to 52 ft and the entrance and jetty channels at Brazos Santiago Pass from 44 ft to 54 ft, making Brownsville the third deepest port in Texas.

For hands-on work — [hull cleaning](/services/hull-cleaning/), [anode replacement](/services/anode-replacement/), clearing a fouled propeller — none of that matters much. A diver's hands find a wasted anode or a rope wrap perfectly well in poor visibility, and close-in video records it.

For a class survey it is decisive. Which is why in-water surveys here are **scheduled**, not squeezed in.

### What actually moves visibility

- **Tide.** Slack water is generally the best window; a running tide stirs the column.
- **Wind.** Sustained onshore wind against a tide is the worst combination.
- **Rainfall.** Freshwater and sediment coming down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande drop clarity for a period afterwards.
- **Nearby dredging or traffic.** A dredge working the same reach, or a deep-draft vessel manoeuvring nearby, will put material in the water column.
- **Location within the port.** A protected berth is not the same water as the open channel, and ABS explicitly prefers a protected location.

None of these is exotic knowledge. All of them are things a crew working this coast every week already tracks, and a crew mobilising three hundred miles for a fixed appointment cannot.

## How a UWILD is actually planned

Three calendars have to line up, and they belong to three different parties.

**Your berth window.** The vessel has to be somewhere it can sit while the examination runs, with the right isolations in place and cargo or bunkering work arranged around it.

**The surveyor.** The attending surveyor has to be available, on site or on the video link, for the duration.

**The water.** See above.

The mistake that costs the most is treating the third as a formality. An owner who books a fixed single morning has committed the surveyor and the berth to a day the weather has not agreed to. If clarity is poor that morning, the choice is to abandon and rebook — paying for everyone's time twice — or to press on and produce an examination the surveyor will not accept.

**Give the job a window.** A two or three day band, with the crew watching conditions and calling the start, converts a coin toss into a manageable job. Where that is genuinely impossible, say so early and we will tell you honestly what the odds look like.

### The sensible sequence

For most owners the efficient route is two steps rather than one.

1. **A condition inspection first.** A commercial examination of the underwater body, on your scope, at your cost, with no surveyor committed. It tells you whether the coatings are sound, the anodes are working, and there is no damage that would require a dry repair.
2. **The UWILD second**, once step one says the vessel is a credible candidate.

If step one finds a problem, you have discovered it cheaply — before booking a survey that would have been abandoned, and while you still have time to plan a docking properly rather than in a hurry. If it finds nothing, you go into the survey knowing what the surveyor is going to see.

## What stops a UWILD, and what happens then

Being straightforward about failure modes is more useful than promising they will not happen.

**Visibility falls below the criterion.** The most common one. The survey is suspended and rescheduled. Nothing is lost except the mobilisation, which is precisely why the window approach matters.

**Marine growth obscures the plate.** You cannot survey what you cannot see. Where growth is the obstacle, cleaning may be a legitimate precursor — but cleaning immediately before a survey has to be discussed with the surveyor rather than assumed, because it changes what the examination can establish about coating condition.

**Damage is found that requires dry repair.** This is not a failed survey. This is the survey working. It has told you, at in-water cost, that the vessel needs a dock — which is a far better outcome than discovering it at the next scheduled docking after a season of trading.

**The society declines to credit the examination.** The reason is almost always eligibility or evidence: the vessel was not a candidate, or the documentation did not meet the requirement. Both are avoidable by settling them in writing before mobilising.

## If your vessel is not a candidate

Sometimes the answer is that an in-water survey is not going to be accepted, and it is worth knowing what that actually means before you treat it as a setback.

Eligibility tightens with age. A society that will credit an in-water survey on a well-maintained vessel in its first special survey periods becomes progressively more cautious as the hull accumulates history, and there is a point past which most societies want the vessel dry. Coating condition matters as much as age: the survey is partly an assessment of protection, and a hull whose coating system has broken down extensively gives the surveyor little to be satisfied about.

Service and type matter too. A vessel in a demanding trade, or one whose survey history includes findings that were deferred, attracts closer scrutiny than one with a clean record.

None of that is a judgement on the vessel. It is a judgement on what an examination through water can reliably establish. And the practical consequence is straightforward: plan the docking properly, with lead time, rather than discovering the constraint the week the survey was due. An owner who asks the question a year out has options. An owner who asks it a month out has a schedule problem.

Where a docking is the outcome, an in-water inspection beforehand still earns its cost. Going into a yard knowing what is there means the specification is written from evidence, the yard quotes against a real scope, and the surprises that turn a docking budget into a docking overrun have mostly been found already.

## What you receive

A UWILD's deliverable has two audiences and has to satisfy both.

**The surveyor** needs the examination to have been conducted and evidenced to the society's requirements: the coverage, the live observation, the recorded imagery of specified items, and the identification of anything found.

**You** need something you can act on afterwards — coating condition, anode state, growth levels, and any defects with enough location and scale detail to plan work around. This part is easy to lose sight of when the survey is the focus, and it is the part that is still useful to you in six months.

Both should reference the vessel's own frame and compartment identifiers, so that a finding can be relocated by somebody who was not present. And the record should state clearly what was **not** examined and why. A report implying coverage it did not achieve is worse than no report, because somebody will eventually rely on it.

## After the survey

Two things are worth doing while the information is fresh, and both are routinely skipped.

**Close out the findings deliberately.** An in-water survey usually turns up items that are not defects but are worth tracking: coating breakdown at a particular area, anodes at a given percentage of consumption, growth levels on the flat of bottom. Those observations are the baseline for the next examination. Filed properly, they turn the next survey into a comparison rather than a fresh start, and comparison is what tells you whether something is progressing or static.

**Set the interval for the routine work.** A survey is a snapshot of condition that has commercial consequences: anodes at two-thirds consumed have a replacement date, and growth at a given level has a [hull cleaning](/services/hull-cleaning/) interval implied by it. Acting on that costs a fraction of what it costs to let both run to the point where the next survey is compromised by them.

The owners who get the most out of in-water surveys treat them as part of a maintenance cycle rather than as a hurdle to clear. The survey establishes condition; the routine work between surveys keeps the vessel in a state where the next one is a straightforward proposition. That is also, incidentally, how a vessel stays an in-water survey candidate for longer.

## The standard the dive itself is held to

Separately from anything class requires, the diving is regulated work. Under **29 CFR 1910 Subpart T**, surface-supplied air diving requires a minimum three-person dive team — a designated person-in-charge, the diver, and a tender — with the diver continuously tended while in the water. Surface-supplied air diving is limited to 190 fsw, with dives of 30 minutes bottom time or less permitted to 220 fsw, and a decompression chamber must be ready at the dive location for any dive outside no-decompression limits or deeper than 100 fsw.

Those are federal minimums that apply to every contractor equally. A survey price that only works with two people on site is a price with the tender left out.

Before anyone enters the water, it is entirely reasonable to ask for the written dive plan, the supervisor's qualifications, the emergency arrangements, and confirmation of how propulsion, thrusters and sea suctions will be isolated and locked out. On a UWILD that last point deserves particular attention, because the examination takes the diver to the running gear and the openings — precisely the machinery that must not start.
