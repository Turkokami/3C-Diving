Salvage is the part of this trade where the temptation to act quickly is strongest and most dangerous. Something has sunk, grounded or come apart, everybody wants it dealt with, and the pressure is to start lifting. Almost every salvage job that goes badly wrong went wrong because a method was chosen before the situation was understood.

This page covers how salvage work is actually approached, what the underwater side contributes, why the assessment is the whole job, and what determines whether something is refloated, lifted or cut up where it lies.

## The assessment comes first, always

Before anyone commits to a method, a set of questions has to be answered. They sound obvious written down and they are routinely skipped under pressure.

**Where is it, exactly?** Position, orientation, depth, and how it sits relative to the channel, the berth and anything else.

**How is it lying?** Upright, listed, inverted, broken. Whether it is resting on the bottom evenly or bridging, which determines what stresses are already in the structure.

**What is the bottom?** Soft silt behaves completely differently from hard bottom. A hull that has settled into soft material may be held by suction far exceeding its own weight, and that suction is a real force that has to be planned for rather than discovered mid-lift.

**Is it stable?** Has it stopped moving, or is it still settling, sliding or working? A hull that is still moving is a hull whose position and condition you do not yet know.

**What is inside it?** Cargo, fuel, oil, hazardous material, machinery, water. This governs the pollution question and it governs weight.

**What is the damage?** Where, how extensive, and whether the hull is capable of holding buoyancy again if dewatered.

**What is at risk nearby?** Other vessels, berth structures, cables, pipelines, the navigable channel.

Only once those are answered can anyone sensibly discuss method. That assessment is largely underwater work, and it is the highest-value thing a dive team contributes to a salvage operation.

## The three broad approaches

**Refloat.** Restore enough buoyancy for the hull to lift itself, by sealing the openings and damage and then dewatering. Fastest and cheapest where it works. It requires the hull to be structurally capable of holding buoyancy once emptied — which a badly damaged, heavily corroded or long-submerged hull may not be. Attempting it on a hull that cannot hold is how you get a partial lift that fails and drops the object in a worse position.

**Lift.** Take the weight externally, with cranes, sheerlegs, lift barges, air bags or a combination. Suits smaller vessels and objects, and situations where refloating is not viable. It requires knowing the weight — including entrained water, cargo, and the bottom suction — and requires rigging points that will actually take the load.

**Cut up in place and recover in pieces.** Where refloating is not possible and lifting whole is impractical. Slow, and it generates its own problems around disposal and around what is released as the structure is opened, but it is sometimes the only realistic route. This makes [underwater cutting](/services/underwater-welding/) a central part of the operation rather than a supporting one.

Most real jobs use elements of more than one. Nearly all of them go through a stabilisation phase first.

## What the dive team does

Our part is the work below the surface, and it runs through the whole job rather than sitting at one point in it.

**Survey and assessment.** Establishing everything in the list above, and documenting it well enough that the people planning the operation, the insurers and the authorities can all rely on it. This is [underwater inspection](/services/underwater-inspection/) work under adverse conditions.

**Stabilisation.** Preventing the situation deteriorating: securing things that may move, closing off what can be closed off, and dealing with immediate pollution risk.

**Sealing and patching.** Closing openings and damage so a hull can hold air or be dewatered. Blanking, patching, and closure work — related to the [overboard blanking](/services/sea-chest-services/) done in routine work but at a different scale and with less control over conditions.

**Rigging.** Getting slings, wires, chains and lifting arrangements around and under an object on the bottom, at the points that will take load without failing or cutting through. Passing rigging beneath a hull settled into soft bottom is among the most demanding work in this trade.

**Cutting.** Access, separation, removing structure that is in the way, and reducing a hull to recoverable sections where that is the chosen method.

**Recovery and clearance.** Bringing the object or its parts up, and confirming afterwards that the bottom is clear — which matters to whoever owns the water.

## Pollution changes the order of everything

Where there is fuel, oil or hazardous cargo aboard, that governs the sequence.

The reason is straightforward: **moving a wreck is the thing most likely to release what is inside it.** A hull that has been stable on the bottom, containing its fuel, can release it the moment it is disturbed. So the assessment of what is aboard and where it is, and the removal or containment of it, has to come before the recovery, not after.

That also means the authorities are involved from the beginning rather than being told afterwards. Where there is pollution potential or a navigation hazard, the Coast Guard has an interest and a role, and the port or terminal whose water it is has both a stake and useful local knowledge.

An owner who is straight about what is aboard from the first conversation gets a better and faster job. An owner who is not creates a situation where the plan has to be rebuilt mid-operation.

## Conditions on this coast

Salvage work here has a particular character, set by three things.

**Visibility and bottom conditions.** The Brownsville Ship Channel carries silt, and freshwater coming down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande after rain reduces clarity further. A great deal of salvage assessment here is done by touch, working systematically and building a picture of an object that cannot be seen as a whole. That takes longer than the same work in clear water and it demands more discipline in the recording, because nothing can be confirmed by a single glance. Silt bottom also means suction is a live consideration on anything that has settled.

**Traffic and the working channel.** This is a busy commercial port — 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years, on a main channel deepened to 52 ft in June 2026, with entrance and jetty channels at 54 ft. A wreck or an obstruction in or near the channel is not just the owner's problem; it affects everybody using it, which adds urgency and adds parties with an interest.

**The recycling and layberth population.** The port captures more than 85% of US Navy and MARAD ship-recycling business, with SteelCoast working an 80-acre complex and around 4,000 ft of main-channel frontage. That produces a steady flow of inactive tonnage arriving under tow, sitting at layberth, and being worked down — and inactive vessels are exactly the population where hull integrity problems, partial sinkings and recovery work arise. Pre-tow condition assessment on tonnage about to be moved is a genuinely useful piece of prevention.

## Stabilisation buys the time to do it properly

Between the assessment and the recovery there is almost always a stabilisation phase, and it is the phase most likely to be skipped under pressure.

The purpose is narrow: stop the situation getting worse while a proper plan is made. That usually means securing anything that could shift, closing off what can be closed, dealing with immediate pollution risk, and where necessary preventing further settling into the bottom.

It is worth doing even when it feels like a delay, for a simple reason. **A situation that is still changing cannot be planned against.** Every hour spent planning a lift on a hull that is still settling is an hour spent planning for a configuration that will not exist by the time the crane arrives. Stabilising first means the plan is made against a known state.

Stabilisation also protects the assessment. Condition as found is the most valuable record in a salvage job and it degrades from the moment anything is disturbed. Work that stops the object moving preserves the evidence of what happened to it, which matters when insurers and lawyers arrive.

There is a commercial dimension too. Stabilisation is comparatively cheap and it converts an urgent problem into a scheduled one. A wreck that is stable, contained and not deteriorating can be recovered next week with the right equipment mobilised, rather than tonight with whatever is available — and the difference in cost between those two operations is usually substantial.

## Hurricane season

Atlantic hurricane season runs 1 June to 30 November, and it is the largest single generator of salvage and recovery work on this coast.

Storm surge moves things that were secured, sinks small craft, drags vessels from moorings, and puts an assortment of material on the bottom around berths. The week after a system passes is the busiest of the year, and the demand arrives all at once — which means capacity across the whole coast is genuinely constrained at exactly the moment everybody needs it.

Two practical consequences. First, preventive work before a system — checking moorings and ground tackle, securing what can be secured, identifying what is likely to become a problem — is worth far more than its cost. Second, having a working relationship with a contractor before you need one is worth something real, because the alternative is joining a queue as an unknown caller.

## Who else is involved, and why that matters

Salvage is rarely a two-party job, and knowing who has an interest saves difficulty.

**The Coast Guard**, where there is pollution potential, a navigation hazard, or a reportable incident. Their involvement is not optional and their timeline may not match yours.

**The port authority or terminal.** It is their water and their berth. They have a legitimate interest in what is on the bottom, how long it stays there, and whether the berth is safe for the next vessel.

**Insurers and their surveyor.** Almost always, and better early. A surveyor involved from the assessment sees the situation as it was; one reading a report weeks later is reconstructing it.

**Class**, if the vessel is classed and there is any prospect of it returning to service.

**A salvage master or coordinating contractor** on larger operations, with the diving as one workstream among several alongside cranes, tugs, barges and pumping.

We work to whoever is coordinating. What we contribute is the underwater picture and the underwater work, and what we will not do is characterise cause or apportion responsibility — our record states what was found, what was done, and what condition things were left in.

## Wreck removal versus salvage

The two get used interchangeably and they have different objectives, which matters because the objective determines the method.

**Salvage** aims to save the property. The vessel, its machinery or its cargo has value worth recovering, and the operation is judged on how much of that value survives. Methods are chosen partly for how gently they treat the object.

**Wreck removal** aims to clear the water. Whether the object survives is secondary or irrelevant; what matters is that it is no longer where it is. This is what a port authority wants when something is obstructing a berth or a channel, and it is frequently what an insurer ends up paying for once a hull is written off.

The distinction changes the engineering. A salvage operation may spend considerable effort sealing and dewatering a hull to lift it intact. A removal operation on the same hull may simply cut it into recoverable sections, which is often faster, cheaper and lower risk precisely because it does not have to preserve anything.

It also changes who is driving. Salvage is usually the owner's or insurer's decision. Removal frequently becomes the port authority's requirement, with a timeline set by their operational needs rather than by the owner's convenience — and where a navigable channel is affected, that timeline can be short.

Being clear which one is being asked for, at the outset, avoids the situation where an operation has been planned and priced for one objective and is then judged against the other.

## Documentation, and why it outlives the job

Salvage generates disputes more reliably than any other marine activity. The record made during the operation is what those disputes get resolved on, sometimes years later.

**Condition as found**, before anything was disturbed, is the single most valuable record and the one most easily lost. Once rigging is passed and a hull is moved, its original state exists only in whatever was documented.

**Sequence and timing.** What was done, in what order, when, and on whose instruction.

**What was observed versus what was inferred.** The position, orientation and visible damage are observations. Why it happened is not, and reports that blur the two get taken apart.

**What could not be established.** Visibility, access, entrapment risk and structural instability all limit what an assessment can determine. Saying so is what makes the rest credible.

## Safety, and the reason salvage diving is different

Routine in-water work happens on a known structure in a known condition. Salvage does not. The hazards are correspondingly different and they compound.

**Instability.** The object may still be moving, and it may move when disturbed. Structure that appears sound may be carrying loads it was never designed for.

**Entrapment.** Wreckage, collapsed structure, cargo, nets and rigging create entrapment hazards that are far worse in poor visibility, where a diver is working by feel in an environment whose shape is not fully known.

**Loads.** Rigging under tension, lifting operations overhead, and the stored energy in a hull held by bottom suction are all serious hazards to somebody in the water beside them.

**Contamination.** Fuel, oil, cargo residues and whatever else is aboard.

**The unknown.** The defining hazard. Every one of the above is manageable when it has been identified, which is precisely why the assessment phase is not a delay before the real work — it is the thing that makes the real work survivable.

The federal standard applies throughout: commercial diving in the United States operates under 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, covering dive team composition, continuous tending, depth limits and decompression provisions. Salvage adds job-specific controls around lifting operations, exclusion zones, and the sequencing of anything that changes the loads while a diver is in the water.
