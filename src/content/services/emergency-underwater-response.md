Underwater emergencies are a scheduling problem as much as a diving problem. Something is wrong below the waterline, it is getting worse or it is stopping the vessel from working, and the useful question is not "what is the theoretically best fix" but **what can be in the water, how soon, and what will that achieve.**

This page sets out what we mobilise for, what to do in the first thirty minutes, how the decision to dive is actually made, and where the honest limits are. If you are dealing with something right now, the number is at the top of this page — call it, and read this afterwards.

## What we mobilise for

**Fouled running gear.** Rope, net, wire or debris around a propeller or shaft, with a vessel that cannot sail or cannot manoeuvre safely. Frequently the fastest job on this list to resolve. See [propeller clearing](/services/propeller-services/).

**Flooding or ingress at the berth.** Water entering the hull through a defect, a failed fitting, damaged plate or a compromised sea connection. The priority is locating the source from outside and slowing it while the crew manages the inside.

**Blocked or damaged sea suctions.** Restricted cooling water is a machinery emergency, and it typically appears as rising temperatures with no obvious internal cause. See [sea chest inspection and repair support](/services/sea-chest-services/).

**Grounding or hard contact.** The vessel touched something and nobody can say what the damage is. Until it is looked at, every decision — sail, stay, tow, dock — is being made blind.

**Dropped objects.** Equipment, tooling, cargo or components over the side, either because they are needed back or because they are now an obstruction at a working berth. See [search and recovery](/services/search-and-recovery/).

**Obstructions at a berth.** Something on the bottom preventing a vessel from coming alongside safely, which is a terminal's problem as much as a vessel's.

**Storm and surge damage.** After weather: what moved, what is now on the bottom, whether moorings and berth structures are still sound, and whether vessels that rode it out took contact.

**Sinking, partial sinking and salvage.** The largest and slowest category, and the one where the first hours of assessment matter most. See [marine salvage and recovery](/services/marine-salvage/).

## The first thirty minutes

What happens before a diver arrives frequently determines how well the job goes.

**Make it safe and stop it getting worse.** Isolate machinery connected to whatever is affected. If something is actively making the situation worse — a pump drawing through a damaged suction, a thruster being used near a suspected wrap — stop it.

**Isolate and lock out, with names on it.** Propulsion, thrusters, sea suctions and overboard discharges connected to the work area must be positively isolated and tagged before anyone can enter the water. Starting this early saves the most time of anything on this list, because it is frequently the step that delays the dive.

**Record what you can see.** Times, what happened in what order, photographs from the deck, soundings, tank levels. This is evidence and it degrades quickly. If insurers or class become involved later, contemporaneous notes made in the first hour are worth more than anything reconstructed afterwards.

**Do not put anyone in the water.** Not a crew member, not a recreational diver. The hazards are machinery, current, entrapment and contaminated water, and none of them are obvious from the deck.

**Call early with a partial picture.** A rough description now is worth more than a complete one in two hours. We can start moving while the picture fills in.

## How the decision to dive is actually made

An emergency does not suspend the standard the work is held to. It changes what is being decided, not how carefully.

Three things get assessed, quickly but properly.

**Can it be done safely?** Current, sea state, visibility, traffic, what is overhead, whether the surface spread can be set up and worked safely at that location, and whether the isolation can be genuinely obtained.

**Will it achieve something?** Diving to look at a problem nobody can fix in the water still has value if it produces a decision. Diving to be seen trying does not.

**Is now better than in six hours?** Sometimes waiting for daylight, slack water or a falling wind produces a faster, safer and cheaper result. Where that is true we will say so. A contractor who never says "wait" is not making an assessment.

Commercial diving in the United States is governed by 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, and its requirements — minimum team size, continuous tending of the diver, depth and chamber provisions — apply exactly as much at two in the morning as at ten in the morning. Urgency does not reduce the team you need. Any contractor who arrives short-handed for an emergency is not doing you a favour.

## What stabilisation means, and what it does not

Most emergency underwater work is **stabilisation**: stopping the situation deteriorating and buying the time to do a proper job.

External patching over a defect. Blanking an opening. Clearing enough of a wrap to get the vessel to a berth where the rest can be done. Securing something that has come adrift. Sealing to reduce ingress to a rate the pumps can hold.

All of these are legitimate and frequently the correct answer. What matters is that **everybody is clear which one is happening.** A temporary measure recorded as a repair is how a vessel ends up trading on something nobody intended to be permanent.

So the deliverable from emergency work is two things: the physical intervention, and an unambiguous record of what was done, what it is expected to hold for, and what still needs doing. Where the answer is a drydock, we say so at the time rather than leaving it implied. See [underwater welding and cutting support](/services/underwater-welding/) for what in-water repair can and cannot achieve.

## What actually delays an emergency dive

Owners are often surprised that the diving is rarely the slow part. Four things delay a job far more reliably, and three of them are within your control.

**Isolation.** The single most common delay. A diver cannot go near a sea suction, a thruster or a propeller until the machinery is positively isolated, locked out, tagged and signed for. On a manned vessel with a chief engineer available that can happen in minutes. On an unmanned barge, a layberthed vessel with no crew aboard, or a terminal structure with a shared system, tracking down whoever can authorise and physically perform the isolation can take longer than the entire dive. Starting it the moment you call is the highest-value thing you can do.

**Access to the water.** A dive spread needs somewhere to sit — quay space, a workboat, or a barge — with a safe route into the water at the right point. A berth face with no access, a vessel lying off, or a location only reachable by boat all add time before anything begins.

**Deciding what is actually being asked.** "Come and look at it" and "come and fix it" mobilise differently. The more precisely the problem is described, the more likely the right people and the right tools arrive the first time.

**Conditions.** The one nobody controls. Current, sea state, traffic and visibility all bear on whether a dive is safe and useful at a given moment.

## Getting the evidence right while it still exists

Emergencies generate legal and commercial consequences long after the immediate problem is dealt with, and the record made in the first hours is what those consequences get decided on.

**Time and sequence.** What happened, in what order, and when. Written down as it happens, not reconstructed afterwards. This is the single most valuable thing a crew can produce and it costs nothing.

**Condition before intervention.** Where it is safe and does not delay anything urgent, documenting the situation as found matters. Once a wrap has been cut away or a patch fitted, the original condition exists only in whatever record was made.

**Separating observation from inference.** "The plate is set in over an area of roughly two metres at frames 34 to 38" is an observation. "The vessel struck the quay" is an inference. Both may be correct; only one is evidence. Reports that blur them get taken apart later.

**What was not examined.** Visibility, access, growth or an isolation that could not be obtained will all limit what an emergency dive can establish. Saying so is not an admission of failure — it is what makes the rest of the report credible.

Where an incident is likely to involve insurers, class or counsel, that documentation standard is the difference between a report that settles a question and one that opens several. See [underwater inspection](/services/underwater-inspection/) for how a full condition record is built when there is time to build one.

## Why local actually matters here

Every contractor claims fast response. The part that is checkable is where the crew and the spread start from.

Mobilisation from the Houston corridor to Brownsville is roughly three hundred miles of road, for people and for a dive spread that has to be loaded, driven and set up. That time is real and it is not compressible. A crew already on this coast starts the clock in a fundamentally different place.

The second part is knowledge that cannot be acquired on the drive down. Which berths have what access. Where a spread can actually be set up. How the tide runs at a given point on the channel. Who to speak to at which terminal to get isolation agreed at night. Those are the things that turn a four-hour job into a two-hour job, and they are accumulated by working somewhere continuously.

The port is busy enough for that to matter: 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years, the only deep-water port directly on the US–Mexico border, with a main channel deepened to 52 ft in June 2026 and entrance and jetty channels at 54 ft. Add substantial LNG construction, a ship-recycling sector capturing over 85% of US Navy and MARAD work, and a roughly 140-boat fishing fleet, and the range of things that can go wrong under the waterline here is unusually wide.

## The categories of emergency, by how they actually behave

Not every underwater emergency has the same shape, and recognising which one you are in changes what the right first move is.

**Getting worse on its own.** Flooding, a failing seal, a defect that is propagating. The clock is real and it is running. Here speed genuinely dominates, and a partial intervention that slows the rate is worth more than a complete one that arrives later.

**Stable but blocking.** A wrap, an obstruction at a berth, a blocked suction on a vessel that is not going anywhere. Nothing is deteriorating, but nothing is happening either. The cost is commercial rather than physical — demurrage, a missed tide, a berth that cannot be used, a boat not fishing. These reward planning slightly more and panic slightly less.

**Already happened, consequences pending.** A grounding, a contact, damage found during another job. Nothing is getting worse, but decisions are waiting on information and every one of them is being made blind until somebody looks. The valuable output here is not an intervention at all — it is a documented assessment good enough for an insurer, a surveyor or a master to act on.

**Unknown.** Something is wrong and nobody can say what. Rising temperatures, an unexplained list, a vibration, a sounding that does not make sense. This is the category where an hour of diving most often saves days, because it converts an unknown into a defined problem with a defined fix.

Saying which of those you are in, when you call, is one of the most useful things you can do. It tells us whether we are racing, planning, or documenting — and those mobilise differently.

## Hurricane season

Atlantic hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November, and on this coast it produces a predictable pattern of emergency work.

**Before a system**, the calls are preventive: mooring and ground tackle checks, confirming berth structures are sound, identifying anything loose on the bottom that could become a projectile or an obstruction.

**After one**, they are diagnostic and they all arrive at once. What moved. What is now on the seabed near a berth. Whether vessels that rode it out took contact. Whether the fenders and pilings that absorbed the surge are still structurally sound. That week is the busiest of the year on this coast, and capacity is genuinely finite — which is an argument for having a contractor you have already worked with rather than one you are calling for the first time.

## Who else needs to be involved

An underwater emergency rarely stays a private matter between an owner and a diving contractor, and thinking about that early saves difficulty later.

**The terminal or port authority.** They control the berth, the access and frequently the isolation. They also have a legitimate interest in anything on the bottom at their facility, and in whether an incident affects the safety of the berth for the next vessel.

**Class.** If the vessel is classed and the damage is structural, or if a repair is made that affects a classed item, the society needs to know. Establishing that at the time is straightforward; explaining it retrospectively is not.

**The Coast Guard.** Certain incidents carry reporting obligations independent of anything else. That is the master's or operator's call, not ours, but it is worth confirming rather than assuming.

**Insurers.** Almost always, and usually sooner than people do it. Insurers appoint surveyors, and a surveyor who is involved from the start gets a far better picture than one reading a report weeks later. It also avoids the situation where work has been done that nobody authorised.

**The charterer.** If the vessel is on charter, the incident has commercial consequences that run in parallel with the physical ones.

We work to whoever is coordinating. What we will not do is characterise a cause, apportion responsibility, or write anything into a report that we did not observe. Our part is what was found, what was done, and what condition it was left in.
