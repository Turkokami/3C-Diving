A port or terminal owns a set of assets it cannot see. The berth face, the piles supporting it, the fenders that absorb every arrival, the bed alongside that determines what can come in, and whatever has accumulated on that bed over the years. All of it affects whether the facility can do its job, and none of it announces a problem until the problem is expensive.

This page covers the work that keeps a terminal operating: what should be on a schedule, what arrives without warning, and why the routine version is so much cheaper than the reactive one.

## What a terminal needs, and when

### On a schedule

**Berth and bed clearance.** Confirming the bottom alongside is clear of debris, dropped material and accumulated obstruction. In a working port this accumulates continuously.

**Depth verification alongside.** Establishing that the depth you declare is the depth you have. Siltation, scour and debris all move it, and the declared figure is a commercial commitment.

**Structure inspection.** Piles, sheet pile, deck structure from below, and the seabed around it. See [pile, dock and pier inspection](/services/pile-dock-pier-inspection/) for what that involves in detail.

**Fender and berthing system checks.** Condition, fixings, chains, and the structure behind them. A failed fender transfers the next berthing straight into the quay.

**Intake and outfall inspection**, where the facility has them.

### Without warning

**Obstruction before an arrival.** Something on the bottom where a vessel needs to be, discovered late.

**After a hard berthing or contact.** The energy went somewhere, and normal use should not resume until somebody knows where.

**Dropped objects** during cargo or maintenance operations. See [search and recovery](/services/search-and-recovery/).

**Post-storm assessment.** What moved, what is now on the bed, and whether the structure took damage.

**Anything affecting whether the berth is safe to use**, which is the question all of the above ultimately reduce to.

## The economics of scheduling it

The argument for putting terminal diving on a schedule is not that it is good practice. It is that the reactive version costs a multiple of the routine one, and the multiple is usually paid in berth time rather than dive time.

A scheduled berth clearance happens in a window you chose, at a time convenient to operations, against a known scope. The same work discovered as an obstruction with a vessel inbound happens at whatever hour it happens, against an unknown scope, with a berth out of use and possibly a vessel waiting. The diving cost differs somewhat. The **cost of the disruption differs enormously.**

The same asymmetry applies to structure. A pile inspected on a cycle produces a maintenance item with a lead time. The same pile inspected after somebody noticed movement produces an urgent engineering assessment, possible load restrictions and a berth whose capacity is uncertain until it is resolved.

For a facility handling meaningful throughput, one avoided day of berth disruption typically covers a year of routine underwater attention. That comparison is worth doing explicitly rather than assuming, because it usually settles the argument.

## Depth alongside is a commercial commitment

This is the item most often left unverified and it is the one with the most direct commercial exposure.

Declared depth alongside determines which vessels a terminal can accept. It appears in port information, in negotiations, and in the assumptions a master makes when bringing a vessel in. And it is not a fixed property of the berth — it is the current state of the bed, which moves.

**Siltation** fills. In a channel carrying the sediment load this one does, with freshwater coming down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande after rain, deposition alongside a berth is continuous rather than occasional.

**Scour** removes, usually in a pattern driven by propeller wash and current, and frequently in exactly the places where the structure needs the ground most.

**Debris** occupies. A single substantial item on the bed can make a nominal depth meaningless at that point.

Establishing depth against a stated datum, and repeating it, is what converts a declared figure into a defensible one. It also tells you the rate — whether a berth is filling, scouring or stable — which is what makes dredging decisions plannable rather than reactive.

That has become more consequential here. The Brazos Island Harbor deepening completed in June 2026, taking the main channel from 42 ft to 52 ft and the entrance and jetty channels from 44 ft to 54 ft, and making Brownsville the third deepest port in Texas. Deeper channel means deeper-draught vessels calling, which means less margin between declared depth and actual keel clearance at a berth — and more energy directed at the bed during manoeuvring.

## Berth clearance before an arrival

Some terminals confirm the bottom is clear ahead of a first call by a new vessel type, or after a period of heavy activity. It is inexpensive and it addresses a specific risk: nobody knows what is on a bed until somebody looks.

What turns up in a working port with this level of activity — 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years — ranges from cargo lost during operations, to dunnage and packaging, to lost mooring lines and wire, to dropped tooling and equipment, to construction material from adjacent work, to items whose origin nobody can explain.

Most of it is harmless. Some of it is not, and the difference matters at the moment a vessel is settling onto it.

Where an obstruction is found, the useful output is not just its removal but a record of what was found and where — because in a shared facility that is frequently the beginning of a conversation about how it got there.

## Intakes and outfalls

Industrial and municipal facilities on the water frequently have intake and outfall structures, and these are underwater assets with the same characteristics as everything else on this page: invisible, degrading, and consequential.

An intake fouls and silts in exactly the way a vessel's [sea chest](/services/sea-chest-services/) does, and the operational consequence is usually larger because the facility behind it is larger. Restricted flow to a cooling system, a process or a treatment plant is a production problem rather than a maintenance one.

The work is inspection, clearing, and condition reporting on the structure itself — screens, gratings, intake heads, and the pipe or culvert as far as it is safely accessible. Where flow is involved the isolation question is more serious than on a vessel, because the volumes are larger and the intake may not have been designed with divers in mind. That gets settled with the facility's engineers before anybody enters the water, and it is the reason this work is planned rather than arranged at short notice.

See [seabed and underwater condition survey](/services/underwater-survey/) where the question extends to the bed and approach conditions around the structure.

## Working alongside operations

Terminal diving happens on a facility that is trying to work, and the coordination is most of what makes it go smoothly.

**A defined working area**, agreed in advance, with everybody who needs to know informed.

**Vessel movement control.** Nothing moves at the berth where divers are in the water. This requires the terminal's active participation, a clear communication route, and everybody knowing who can stop it.

**Isolation of anything relevant** within the working area — intakes, pumps, and any machinery that could operate.

**Awareness of what is happening above.** Cargo operations, crane movement and vehicle traffic on the quay all bear on the safety of people working below it.

In practice, terminals that do this regularly develop a routine and the work becomes straightforward. Terminals arranging it for the first time usually find the coordination takes longer than the diving, which is a good argument for establishing the arrangement before there is any urgency attached to it.

## Moorings, dolphins and mooring points

The parts of a facility that hold vessels in place get less attention than the parts vessels lie against, and they fail with less warning.

**Mooring dolphins** are structures like any other and degrade the same way, but they are loaded differently: they take horizontal load in tension rather than the compressive impact a fender face absorbs. That means the connections and the foundation matter more than the surface, and a dolphin whose piles have lost embedment to scour is a different proposition from a quay wall that has.

**Anchor points, bollard foundations and their below-water connections** are frequently original to the facility and frequently never examined. Where the loads have increased — larger vessels, higher windage, more powerful tugs assisting — the original design assumptions may no longer describe what the structure is being asked to do.

**Ground tackle and mooring systems** in the water, where a facility uses buoys, chains or anchors. Chain wears at the touchdown point and at any fairlead, and the wear is not visible from the surface. Anchors move. Connections corrode.

**What holds it all to the bottom.** For any moored system, the seabed component is the part nobody sees at all, and it is where a failure produces the most consequence — a vessel adrift in a working channel rather than an inconvenience at a berth.

The same argument applies here as everywhere else on this page. Inspected on a cycle, these are maintenance items with lead times. Discovered at failure, they are incidents.



A terminal's underwater records serve a different purpose from a vessel's. They are asset management records, and they are read by people making budget and capacity decisions over years.

**Consistent referencing.** Findings located against the facility's own numbering — berth, bay, pile, chainage — so records from different years describe the same things the same way.

**Condition described rather than graded**, with dimensions where they matter, so that a later comparison is possible.

**Bed levels against a stated datum**, repeated, so that siltation and scour appear as rates rather than as isolated observations.

**A clear separation between observation and inference.** Particularly where damage is found, and particularly where more than one party operates at the facility.

**An explicit statement of coverage** — what was examined, what was not, and why.

Over several cycles that produces something genuinely valuable: a picture of how the facility is changing, which is what allows maintenance to be planned against condition rather than against a calendar or against the last unpleasant surprise.

## Shared facilities and the question of who pays

At a multi-user facility, underwater findings raise a question that a single-owner berth does not: whose problem is this?

Debris on the bed at a berth used by several operators, damage to a fender that could have come from any of a dozen arrivals, or an obstruction whose origin nobody will claim all create the same difficulty. The facility wants it resolved. Nobody wants to fund it.

What resolves these in practice is the record, and specifically the record made routinely rather than after the argument started. A berth inspected on a cycle produces a documented condition at a series of dates. When something is found, its appearance can be bracketed between two known-good inspections, which narrows the question considerably. A berth inspected only when there is a dispute produces a single observation with no history, and the argument becomes about interpretation.

The same logic applies to damage after a specific arrival. A structure whose condition was documented last month gives a clear basis for attributing new damage. One whose condition was last documented at construction does not.

We do not adjudicate any of this and we will not characterise how something got where it is. What we provide is what was found, where, in what condition, on a stated date, alongside whatever the previous record showed. That is the material the parties need to sort it out between themselves, and having it usually shortens the conversation considerably.

## Response work

When something happens at a berth, the question is almost always the same: **can we keep using it?**

Answering that quickly is worth more than answering it exhaustively. A rapid assessment establishing that the structure is undamaged and the bed is clear puts a berth back into service. A rapid assessment finding something means the restriction is based on evidence rather than caution.

What we would want to know when you call: what happened, which berth, what was involved, whether anything is visibly affected above water, and whether the berth is currently in use. For anything time-critical, see [emergency underwater response](/services/emergency-underwater-response/).

## Safety on terminal work

Terminal diving carries hazards that vessel work does not.

**Vessel movements** are the controlling one, covered above.

**Intakes and discharges** within the working area, which can start without warning unless positively isolated.

**Overhead operations** — cranes, cargo handling, vehicle traffic — with a diver below.

**Structure and debris** presenting entrapment risk, worsened by the poor visibility typical of this channel.

**Multiple parties** operating in the same space, which is why a single agreed control regime matters more here than on a vessel with one master.

Commercial diving in the United States is governed by 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, which fixes the minimum dive team, continuous tending of the diver, depth limits and decompression provisions for every contractor equally. At a terminal, the additional planning effort goes into the interface with facility operations, and it is worth asking any contractor how they propose to control vessel movement before they are in the water.

## The recycling and layberth sector

The Port of Brownsville has a characteristic that makes its terminal diving requirement genuinely unusual: it captures more than 85% of US Navy and MARAD ship-recycling business, with SteelCoast alone operating an 80-acre complex and roughly 4,000 ft of water frontage on the main channel.

That produces a distinct pattern of work that most ports do not generate.

**Tow-in arrivals.** Vessels arriving under tow, often inactive for a long period, whose underwater condition is frequently unknown to everybody including the party receiving them. Establishing the state of a hull before it is brought alongside is a reasonable precaution for the facility as much as for the owner.

**Layberth tonnage.** Vessels sitting for extended periods are stationary substrates that foul heavily, and their sea connections, anodes and hull integrity go unattended by definition. Where such a vessel is going to be moved, its condition is part of establishing whether it is fit to move.

**Working berths.** Vessels being cut down produce steel, tooling and material in the water in a way an ordinary cargo berth does not. Periodic bed clearance around cutting berths is a genuine operational requirement rather than a precaution.

**Dropped objects and recovery**, at a scale and frequency that reflects the nature of the work.

None of this is exotic diving. It is ordinary terminal work with an unusual driver, and the point of raising it is that a facility in this sector has a different underwater maintenance profile from a container or bulk terminal of the same size — and a schedule copied from one will not fit the other.
