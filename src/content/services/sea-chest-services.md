Sea chests are the least glamorous item on a vessel and among the most consequential. They are simply openings in the hull that let seawater in for cooling, ballast, firefighting and general service — and when they stop delivering, the consequences are immediate and mechanical rather than gradual and economic.

This page covers what goes wrong inside a sea chest, how it is found and cleared, what overboard blanking makes possible, and why the coordination between the dive team and the vessel's engineers is most of the job.

## Why sea chests cause disproportionate trouble

Three things combine.

**They are sheltered, so they foul faster.** A sea chest is a recess in the hull with reduced flow and no exposure to the scouring effect the open hull gets under way. It is a good place to be a barnacle. So the fouling rate inside a chest routinely exceeds that on the surrounding plate.

**Their failure mode is operational, not economic.** A fouled hull costs fuel. A restricted sea chest costs cooling water, and cooling water is what stands between machinery and damage. The consequence is not a slightly worse quarter, it is rising temperatures and a decision about whether to keep running.

**Nobody looks at them.** They are out of sight from the deck and out of scope on a lot of hull work. A cleaning job specified as "the hull" frequently does not include them, and so they are the part of the underwater body most likely to be several years past attention.

The Gulf makes all of this worse. Warm, biologically productive water with a long growing season means faster fouling than the same vessel would see in a colder trade — and idle time compounds it, which matters in a port with substantial layberth activity.

## What we find

**Marine growth on internal surfaces and gratings.** The most common finding, and the one that restricts flow. Growth on the grating bars reduces the open area directly; growth inside the chest reduces it further.

**Silt and debris.** The Brownsville Ship Channel carries silt, and material settles into a low-flow recess. Plastic, line and general debris get drawn onto gratings, particularly after weather.

**Wasted or missing anodes.** Sea chest anodes are protecting an area that is both operationally important and completely unseen, and they are consistently the anodes found in the worst condition — frequently gone entirely, with nobody aware. See [anode replacement and cathodic protection](/services/anode-replacement/).

**Coating breakdown and corrosion.** Chest internals are a difficult coating environment. Breakdown leads to corrosion of the chest walls and grating bars, and heavily wasted grating bars are both a flow problem and a structural one.

**Damaged or distorted gratings.** From impact, from ice in other trades, from previous removal and refitting, or simply from corrosion having taken enough material.

**Blocked or partially blocked strainer plates**, where fitted.

## Clearing and inspection

The straightforward job is inspection and clearing, and it can usually be done without removing anything.

The diver assesses the grating and the accessible interior, records the condition, and clears growth and debris from the grating and the chest as far as access allows. Where the restriction is at the grating — which it often is — that alone can restore the flow.

Where the problem is deeper inside, or where the grating itself is wasted or damaged, grating removal becomes part of the scope. That is a materially bigger operation: it opens the chest, so the system has to be properly isolated first, and it puts the vessel in a condition it should not stay in longer than necessary.

The other reason to look inside is diagnostic. If a vessel has recurring cooling problems, establishing whether the cause is the intake, the chest, the strainer or something further into the system saves a great deal of engineer time spent eliminating possibilities in the wrong order.

## Overboard blanking, and what it makes possible

This is the capability that turns a docking job into a berth job, and it is worth understanding.

Many repairs to seawater systems — a valve, a strainer, a section of pipe, a fitting — require the system to be opened. Opening a system connected to the sea, with the vessel afloat, admits the sea. **Blanking** solves this: a plate is fitted and sealed over the hull opening from outside, so the connection is closed at the shell and everything inboard of it can be drained and worked on dry.

The sequence is a joint operation and the sequence itself is the safety case:

1. The system is isolated inboard by the vessel's engineers, valves shut and secured, with named accountability.
2. The diver fits and secures the blank over the opening from outside, and it is confirmed effective.
3. The system inboard is drained and the work is carried out by the engineers or the repair contractor.
4. The work is completed, the system is made up and confirmed ready.
5. The blank is removed, and the system is restored and tested.

Every one of those steps has to be agreed before any of them begins, because the failure modes are severe in both directions. A blank removed before the system is made up floods a compartment. A system opened before the blank is confirmed does the same. Cofferdam arrangements work on the same principle where the geometry needs a different approach.

This is why we treat sea chest work as a planning job first and a diving job second. The dive is usually the shortest part.

## Working with the vessel's engineers

More than any other service we offer, this one is done jointly rather than alongside.

The engineers own the system, know its arrangement, control the isolation, and are responsible for what happens inboard. We control what happens at the shell. The interface between those two is where things go wrong if it has not been talked through.

What makes it work in practice:

- **A shared understanding of the arrangement** — which chest serves which system, what valves isolate it, and what else is affected when it is shut down.
- **Agreement on the sequence and who authorises each step**, written down rather than assumed.
- **Clear communication during the work**, so nobody acts on a belief about the state of the system that is out of date.
- **An explicit handover at each transition**, particularly before the blank comes off.

Where a vessel is unmanned, layberthed, or under a superintendent rather than a crew, that coordination takes longer to arrange and should be started earlier. This is a common situation in a port with the layberth and recycling activity Brownsville has.

## What a sea chest actually serves

It is worth being concrete about why a restriction matters, because "sea chest" is an abstraction and the systems behind it are not.

**Main engine and auxiliary cooling.** The one that produces the emergency. Restricted flow means rising temperatures, and rising temperatures mean a decision about whether to keep running. On a vessel under way that decision is made at sea.

**Generator cooling.** Same physics, different consequence — this one takes electrical supply with it.

**Ballast.** A restricted intake makes ballast operations slow rather than dangerous, but slow ballast operations affect cargo scheduling, draught management and turnaround.

**Firefighting and general service.** The consequence here is availability rather than performance, and it is the one nobody notices until the day it matters. A fire pump that cannot draw is a serious deficiency and it will not announce itself during normal operations.

**Fresh water generation and other services**, depending on the vessel.

The point of listing them is that "the sea chest is a bit fouled" describes different levels of urgency depending on what is behind it. When an intake is found restricted, the question that follows is which systems it serves and what the vessel's redundancy actually is — because a vessel with two chests cross-connected is in a very different position from one where a single intake serves everything that matters.

That question is also what determines how quickly the work needs to happen, and it is worth having the answer before the enquiry rather than working it out at the berth.

## Low sea chests, high sea chests, and silt

Many vessels have both a low and a high sea suction, and the reason is directly relevant to working at this port.

The **low suction** draws from further down the hull and is the normal choice in open water. The **high suction** draws nearer the waterline and exists for shallow, silty or debris-laden water — precisely because drawing from low down in such conditions pulls sediment straight into the system.

The Brownsville Ship Channel is working water that carries silt, and freshwater coming down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande after rain adds to the sediment load. Vessels operating here, and particularly those sitting for extended periods, accumulate material in low chests at a rate that surprises crews used to cleaner water.

Two practical consequences follow. First, an inspection should establish the condition of **both** intakes, not just whichever one is currently in use — the one being held in reserve is the one whose condition nobody knows, and it is being relied on precisely for the situation where the other is compromised. Second, silt accumulation is a maintenance item rather than a defect: it will happen, and the useful thing is knowing the rate so the clearing can be planned.

## Repair support

Where the chest or grating itself needs work, in-water repair is sometimes possible and sometimes not.

Grating repair or replacement, doubler plates over wasted areas, and attachment work are all candidates for in-water welding — with the weld class defined in advance according to what the item does. A grating is not a primary structural member, but it is a component whose failure has consequences, and the standard should be stated rather than assumed. See [underwater welding and cutting support](/services/underwater-welding/).

Extensive wastage of chest walls is different. That is a plate renewal question, and plate renewal in a confined recess is a drydock job. Where an inspection finds that, the useful output is a clear description of extent and location so the docking can be specified properly rather than explored on arrival.

## How this fits the local traffic

The Port of Brownsville moved 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years, on a main channel deepened to 52 ft in June 2026, with entrance and jetty channels at 54 ft. Three local patterns generate sea chest work.

**Deep-draft commercial tonnage** on a schedule, where the work is planned into a berth window and the priority is not disrupting cargo operations.

**Layberth and inactive tonnage.** With the port capturing over 85% of US Navy and MARAD ship recycling, there is a significant population of vessels sitting for long periods. Sea chests on inactive vessels foul heavily and their anodes are frequently gone. Where such a vessel is to be moved or towed, the state of its sea connections is part of establishing whether it is fit to go.

**The fishing and workboat fleet.** Smaller systems, tighter margins, and a strong preference for problems being solved today rather than scheduled. The roughly 140-boat Brownsville–Port Isabel shrimp fleet generates steady small-scale intake work.

## Planning a blanking job so it does not overrun

Blanking jobs go wrong in scheduling far more often than in execution, and almost always for the same reasons. Getting three things settled in advance removes most of the risk.

**Confirm the opening before the day.** The blank has to fit and seal against the actual shell geometry around the opening, which means knowing the size, the shape, the condition of the surrounding plate and whether there is anything — growth, a damaged edge, a protruding fitting — that will prevent a good seal. An inspection dive before the repair is booked is cheap insurance and frequently changes the plan.

**Agree the duration inboard, honestly.** The blank stays on while the internal work happens, and the vessel is in an abnormal condition for the whole of that period. If the inboard work is expected to take four hours and takes two days, that has consequences for everybody. Overestimating is far better than the alternative.

**Establish who is on site for the removal.** A blank has to come off, and it comes off only when the system inboard is confirmed made up and ready. That requires the diving crew and the engineers to be available at the same moment, at the end of a job whose finishing time was uncertain. Jobs that run into the evening and find the dive crew released hours earlier are a common and entirely avoidable problem.

Where the internal work is being done by a third-party repair contractor rather than the vessel's own engineers, all three of these need agreeing across three parties rather than two. That coordination is genuinely most of the job, and it is worth starting it well before anybody needs to be at the berth.

## Safety

Sea chest work concentrates several serious hazards in one place.

**Suction is the defining one.** A sea suction that starts while a diver is at the grating can trap them against it. Positive isolation of the relevant pumps and systems, locked out and tagged with named accountability and confirmed physically, is not a procedural nicety — it is the entire safety case for the job. Nothing proceeds without it.

**Enclosed and confined space considerations** apply once a chest is opened, and they apply to whoever is working inboard as much as to the diver.

**Where hot work is involved near an opening**, the potential for gas accumulation in a partially enclosed space is assessed before rather than discovered during.

On top of that, commercial diving in the United States is regulated under 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, which fixes the minimum dive team, continuous tending, depth limits and chamber requirements for every contractor equally. Ask for the job-specific dive plan, the supervisor's qualifications, the isolation arrangement and who signs it, and the emergency plan for that berth.

## Getting sea chests onto the schedule

The simplest improvement most operators can make is to stop treating sea chests as a separate job.

Add them to the scope of every hull visit. The diver is already on the hull, the marginal cost of assessing the intakes is small, and it converts the most operationally consequential part of the underwater body from something checked when it fails into something tracked continuously. Recording growth level and anode condition each visit builds the same kind of trend data that makes [hull cleaning](/services/hull-cleaning/) intervals predictable.

Tell us the vessel, which systems have been giving trouble if any, whether you have arrangement drawings, and whether the vessel is manned. If a repair is contemplated, tell us who is doing the inboard work so the sequence can be agreed between all parties before anybody mobilises.
