On a marine construction project the dive team is a small line in the budget and a large one in the programme. Everything below the waterline goes through them, and when something under water is not as expected, the whole job waits.

This page covers what dive support actually contributes across a construction programme, how the interface with lifting operations is managed, and why bringing divers in early is consistently cheaper than bringing them in when something has gone wrong.

## Where dive support fits in a programme

**Before construction.** Surveying the bed and any existing structure, identifying obstructions, establishing what is actually there against what the drawings say. This is the phase most often compressed and the one with the best return.

**During installation.** Guiding and checking piles and structure into position, verifying set and alignment, rigging and de-rigging, supporting template arrangements, and dealing with whatever is found on the way down.

**Throughout.** Cutting and burning, clearing obstructions, recovering dropped equipment, and inspecting work as it is completed rather than at the end.

**On completion.** As-built verification below the waterline, and a baseline record of the finished structure and the bed around it.

That last item is worth emphasising because it is routinely treated as optional. See [pile, dock and pier inspection](/services/pile-dock-pier-inspection/) for why a baseline is worth so much more than the inspection that produces it.

## Why early is cheaper

The single most expensive thing on a marine construction site is plant standing idle, and the most common reason for it below the waterline is an unexpected obstruction.

The Port of Brownsville has a long working history and substantial current development — Rio Grande LNG building toward 48 MTPA with two ship berths and a turning basin in its first phase, Texas LNG progressing a second terminal, and a channel deepened in June 2026 to 52 ft with entrance and jetty channels at 54 ft. New work regularly goes in where old work already was.

What turns up: **old piles cut off below the mudline** and forgotten, **abandoned structure** from previous facilities, **debris and dropped material** accumulated over decades, **existing services** not where the drawings put them, and **rock or hard material** at a different level than the survey suggested.

Finding any of those with a crane on hire, a piling rig on site and a barge crew waiting is an expensive discovery. Finding it during a pre-construction survey costs a fraction of that and usually changes the method statement rather than the programme.

The same logic applies to as-built verification. Confirming that installed work matches the design **while the contractor is still mobilised** means any correction happens with the plant already there. Establishing it after demobilisation means bringing everything back.

## Working with lifting operations

The interface between diving and lifting is where marine construction gets dangerous, and it is managed by protocol rather than by care.

**Nothing moves while a diver is in a position to be caught by it.** No lifting, no slewing, no lowering, no repositioning of a barge or spud. This is absolute and it is not subject to schedule pressure.

**Communication runs through a defined route.** The dive supervisor and the crane or lift supervisor have an agreed channel and an agreed protocol, and everyone knows who can stop the job. Anybody can stop it; only defined people can restart it.

**The sequence is agreed before anything begins.** Who does what, in what order, and what the trigger is for each transition. Improvising this at the rail while a load is in the air is how people get hurt.

**Exclusion zones are physical and understood**, not notional.

In practice this means the dive team and the lifting team plan together rather than in parallel. On a well-run job the toolbox talk covers the interface explicitly and everybody can state what happens if the diver loses communication. On a badly run one, the diving is treated as a subcontract that happens somewhere near the lifting.

We will not work to the second arrangement, and it is worth knowing that up front rather than discovering it at the site.

## Piling support

Piling is where dive support most often ends up on the critical path, because the dive team is frequently the only party who can say what is actually happening at the bottom.

**Guiding and positioning.** Getting a pile into a template or onto its position, particularly in poor visibility where surface indications are limited.

**Checking set and alignment.** Verifying position and verticality against the design during and after driving, when correction is still possible.

**Assessing refusal.** When a pile stops going down before the design penetration, the question is why. An obstruction, a hard layer, or damage to the pile toe are different problems with different answers, and a diver at the bottom can often distinguish them where surface data cannot.

**Damage inspection.** Piles get damaged during driving. Establishing what happened, and whether it matters, before the rig moves off.

**Template and frame support.** Setting, checking, adjusting and recovering the arrangements that hold everything in position.

**Cutting.** Cropping piles to level, removing obstructions, and cutting away temporary works. See [underwater welding and cutting support](/services/underwater-welding/).

## Working conditions here

**Visibility.** The Brownsville Ship Channel carries silt, and construction activity itself puts material into the water column — excavation, driving and barge movement all reduce clarity around the work. Marine construction diving here is substantially tactile work, and that is normal rather than exceptional. It does affect certain tasks: fine alignment checks and detailed visual verification take longer and sometimes need conditions to be allowed to settle.

**The bed.** Silt bottom means objects settle into it, which matters both for finding things and for the suction encountered when lifting them. It also means excavations and dredged profiles move.

**Traffic.** This is a working commercial port, not a closed site. Construction happens alongside a channel carrying 28 million tons of cargo a year, up 101% over five years. Vessel movements, wash and traffic management are part of the daily planning rather than an occasional interruption.

**Weather and season.** Atlantic hurricane season runs 1 June to 30 November. For a construction programme this means both schedule risk and a set of preparatory obligations — securing plant, moorings and partially completed work before a system, and assessing what moved afterwards.

## What a dive team can and cannot do quickly

Programmes get built on assumptions about diving durations, and the assumptions are frequently wrong in both directions. Being explicit helps everybody plan.

**Genuinely fast.** Checking a known item at a known location. Connecting or releasing rigging at an accessible point. A quick look at something specific when the diver is already in the water. Recovering a dropped item whose position was marked.

**Slower than people expect.** Anything systematic. A search over an area, a verification of a run of installed work, or an inspection covering a defined extent all take time proportional to the area, and in poor visibility that time goes up considerably. Fine alignment work by touch is slow. Anything in strong current is slow, and sometimes has to wait for the tide.

**Not compressible at all.** The dive team requirements under the standard. Decompression obligations, where depth and duration bring them into play. And the time to set up and break down a spread, which is fixed regardless of how short the actual task is — a five-minute job still needs the full mobilisation.

That last point drives a practical piece of advice: **batch the underwater work.** A programme that calls divers back four times for four small tasks pays four mobilisations. One that groups them into a single visit pays one. Where tasks genuinely cannot be batched, that is fine — but it should be a decision rather than an accident of sequencing.

The corollary is worth saying too. If something needs checking under water and it is not on the programme, tell us while we are there rather than after we have demobilised. The marginal cost of an extra task during an existing dive is a fraction of the cost of coming back for it.

## Working alongside other trades

Dive support rarely operates alone. On a typical job the interfaces are with the piling contractor, the crane and lifting operation, the barge and marine plant operators, the surveyors setting out and verifying position, the client's engineer or resident representative, and frequently a dredging operation.

Two things make those interfaces work.

**Being on the programme, not adjacent to it.** Dive tasks have durations, dependencies and weather sensitivity like anything else. A programme that shows "divers" as a resource rather than as sequenced activities will find the diving becomes the constraint at the worst moment.

**Being clear about what diving cannot do quickly.** Some tasks are genuinely fast. Others — a systematic search, a detailed verification, work in confined conditions or strong current — are not, and no amount of pressure changes that. A contractor who tells you a task will take a shift when it will take three is not helping the programme.

## Dredging and excavation support

A substantial share of marine construction on this coast involves moving material rather than installing structure, and diving supports that differently.

**Pre-dredge survey.** Establishing what is actually on the bed before a dredger starts. This matters more than it sounds: a dredge that encounters an obstruction can be damaged by it, and an obstruction found by a cutter head is considerably more expensive than one found by a diver.

**Obstruction clearing.** Removing what the dredge cannot handle — old piles, debris, abandoned structure, dropped plant. Frequently the reason a dive team is on a dredging job at all.

**Verification of profile.** Confirming that the dredged section matches what was specified, particularly at edges, slopes and around structures where the plant cannot reach cleanly.

**Support to the plant itself.** Inspecting and clearing cutter heads, suction inlets and pipelines, checking spuds and anchoring arrangements, and recovering equipment lost over the side.

**Protecting adjacent structures.** Dredging next to an existing berth changes the ground supporting it. Monitoring bed levels around structures during and after the work is how you find out whether it has undermined anything, and it is far better to know at the time than at the next structural inspection.

That last point connects directly to scour, and it is worth raising early on any job where excavation is happening near existing infrastructure. The bed profile a structure was designed against is part of its structural condition, and changing it is a structural change even when nobody touched the structure.

## Documentation on a construction job

Construction generates disputes, and underwater work is disproportionately represented in them because so few people saw it.

**What was found before work started.** The pre-construction record is what establishes whether an obstruction was a pre-existing condition or something introduced.

**What was installed and where.** Position, level, alignment, against the design and against a stated datum.

**Condition at completion**, with imagery.

**What was cut, removed or altered**, and what was left in place.

**Deviations, and what was done about them.** The most valuable entries in any construction record.

Where the client's engineer has a required format, we report into it. Where they do not, the standard is the same as for any inspection: located against the structure's own references, described rather than graded, and explicit about what was not examined.

## Commissioning and handover

The end of a marine construction job has an underwater component that is frequently squeezed, because by that stage everybody wants to demobilise.

**Final as-built verification.** Confirming the completed work below the waterline matches the design — position, level, alignment, connections, and that temporary works have actually been removed rather than assumed removed. Cut-off piles, template fixings, guide frames and rigging left behind become somebody's obstruction later, and they are far easier to deal with now.

**Clearance of the working area.** Confirming the bed around the completed structure is clear of dropped material, offcuts and debris. On a job that has run months, the accumulation is usually greater than anyone expects.

**Bed levels as left.** The profile around a new structure at handover is the baseline every future scour assessment is measured against. Establishing it while the survey team is still on site costs very little; reconstructing it in three years is impossible.

**The condition record.** A documented state of the finished work at handover, which is what protects the contractor as much as the client. It establishes what was delivered and in what condition, at a date, before anybody else has operated on it.

The last point is worth making to contractors directly: this record is at least as valuable to you as to your client. Damage discovered eighteen months later is a much harder conversation without evidence of the condition at handover, and that evidence only exists if somebody made it.

## Safety on a construction site

Marine construction concentrates more simultaneous hazards than any other work in this trade, and they interact.

**Lifting operations overhead**, covered above.

**Plant and machinery** — excavators, hammers, spuds and barge movement — all producing loads, noise, vibration and the potential for sudden movement near a diver.

**Entrapment** among structure, temporary works, rigging and debris, in water where it cannot be seen developing.

**Changing conditions.** A construction site changes hour to hour. What was true at the morning briefing may not be true after lunch, which is why the dive plan is reviewed as the site changes rather than written once.

**Multiple parties**, each with their own operations, which is why a single agreed control regime matters more here than anywhere else.

Commercial diving in the United States operates under 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, which sets the minimum dive team, continuous tending of the diver, depth limits and decompression provisions. Those requirements do not flex for programme pressure. A contractor offering to work a shift pattern that cannot be resourced within them is offering something they should not.

## Working in a live commercial port

Construction here does not happen on a closed site, and that shapes the job more than most contractors expect coming in.

**The channel keeps working.** The port moved 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years, and it is the only deep-water port directly on the US-Mexico border. Deep-draught vessels transit past construction sites on a schedule that has nothing to do with the programme, and their wash arrives whether or not it is convenient.

**Terminals keep operating.** Work adjacent to an active berth means coordinating with cargo operations that have their own priorities, their own safety regime and their own view of who has access to what.

**Permissions involve more parties.** The port authority, individual terminal operators, and where navigation is affected, the Coast Guard. Establishing early who has to agree to what avoids the situation where a method statement is complete and the permission to execute it is not.

**Windows are shared.** The same tide state that suits a construction operation frequently suits a vessel movement, and the vessel movement usually wins.

None of this is unusual for a working port and none of it is an obstacle. It is a planning input, and it is one where a crew that works this water continuously has a genuine advantage over one mobilising in — not because the diving differs, but because knowing who to call and what a given berth is like on a falling tide removes delays that are otherwise discovered the hard way.

## Bringing us in

Tell us the project, the programme, what is going in the water and where, and what surveys or drawings exist. If there is a method statement, send it — most of what we would ask is answerable from it, and a good deal of what we would suggest concerns the sequence rather than the diving.

The most useful conversation happens before the plant is mobilised. A pre-construction survey is a small cost against a programme, and it is the single most reliable way to avoid the discovery that stops a job with everything on hire and nothing to do.
