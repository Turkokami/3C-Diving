An underwater inspection answers a question you cannot answer from the deck: what is actually happening to the part of your asset that is under water. On a vessel that means the hull, the running gear, the sea chests and the appendages. On fixed infrastructure it means the pilings, the fenders, the sheet pile and the seabed around them. The work is the same in principle — get eyes and a camera on the structure, record what is there, and hand back something you can make a decision with.

This page covers how that work is actually done on the Brownsville Ship Channel, what changes here compared with clearer water, what you receive at the end, and how an owner's inspection differs from a class survey. It is deliberately specific about constraints, because the constraints are what decide whether an inspection is worth what you paid for it.

## What an underwater inspection covers

The scope depends on why you are asking. Four requests come up again and again on this coast.

**General condition.** You want to know the state of the underwater body — coating condition, fouling, corrosion, anode wastage, obvious mechanical damage. This is the inspection that feeds a maintenance plan and a drydock budget. It is also the one most often skipped until something forces it.

**Something specific happened.** A grounding, a hard contact with a berth, a suspected strike, a vibration that started on a particular voyage. Here the inspection is diagnostic: the question is not "what condition is the vessel in" but "what did that event do, and can we sail." The scope narrows and the documentation standard rises, because the answer is going to an insurer, a surveyor, or both.

**Pre-purchase or pre-charter.** Someone is about to take on a liability and wants it quantified first. This inspection is written for a reader who was not there and has no reason to take anyone's word for it.

**Statutory or class-driven.** A survey is due and the owner wants to know whether the vessel is a credible candidate for an in-water survey rather than a drydocking. See [UWILD and in-water survey support](/services/uwild-in-water-survey/) — the requirements are different and they are set by the class society, not by us.

### On a vessel

A full underwater examination works through the flat of bottom, the bilge keels, the boot top and sides, then the running gear and appendages: propeller blades and hub, rudder and pintles, stern tube and seal area, bow and stern thruster tunnels and grids, stabiliser fins where fitted. Then the openings — sea chests, strainer gratings, overboard discharges, transducers and the log — and finally the sacrificial anodes, which are the quickest read on whether the vessel's [cathodic protection](/services/anode-replacement/) is doing its job.

### On fixed structures

For berths, docks, piers and intakes the work covers pile condition above and below the mudline where accessible, connections and bracing, fender and dolphin systems, sheet pile and tie-backs, scour and undermining at the base, and debris or obstruction on the berth face. See [pile, dock and pier inspection](/services/pile-dock-pier-inspection/) for how that scope is structured, and [seabed and underwater condition survey](/services/underwater-survey/) where the question is the bottom itself rather than the structure.

## How inspection actually works in the Brownsville Ship Channel

This is where a local contractor is worth more than a cheaper one from further up the coast, and it is worth being concrete about why.

### The channel is deeper than most people's information

The Brazos Island Harbor Channel Improvement Project completed on 25 June 2026. The main channel went from 42 ft to **52 ft**, and the entrance and jetty channels at Brazos Santiago Pass from 44 ft to **54 ft**. That makes Brownsville the third deepest port in Texas.

It is worth stating plainly because a great deal of published material — including material written well after the fact — still quotes the old 42 ft figure. If a contractor's own site tells you the channel is 42 ft, they are working from stale information about the most basic fact of the port.

For inspection work the deepening matters in two practical ways. Deeper draught vessels now call here, which means more underwater body to cover and more of it further down. And the dredging and construction that produced the new depth has kept a working amount of fine material moving in the water column.

### Visibility is the real constraint, and it is manageable

The channel carries silt. On an ordinary day you are working close in, with the camera near the plate and the light angled to avoid backscatter. A great deal of genuinely useful inspection is done this way — a diver's hands find pitting, weld cracking, a wasted anode or a rope wrap perfectly well, and close-in video records it.

What poor visibility does constrain is **class work**. ABS requires the water to be clear enough to show the full height of the propeller and rudder in a single frame, and in general expects an in-water survey to be conducted somewhere protected with reasonably calm, clear water. Visibility of roughly two metres or better is the practical benchmark.

That single criterion is why in-water surveys here are scheduled rather than improvised. Slack water, wind direction, recent rainfall through the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande, and whether a dredge is working nearby all move visibility. A contractor who tells you visibility is never a problem is either not doing class work or not telling you the truth about it.

### The vessel mix here is not generic

The Port of Brownsville moved 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years, and it is the only deep-water port directly on the US–Mexico border. Three local fleets generate distinct inspection work.

**Ship recycling and layberth tonnage.** The port captures more than 85% of US Navy and MARAD ship-recycling business, and SteelCoast alone runs an 80-acre complex with roughly 4,000 ft of water frontage on the main channel. Vessels arrive under tow, sit at layberth, and are worked down. That produces pre-tow condition surveys, hull integrity checks on inactive tonnage, cofferdam and blanking support, and dropped-object recovery around cutting berths. It is a genuinely unusual concentration of work and it does not exist in most ports.

**LNG construction traffic.** Rio Grande LNG is building toward 48 MTPA across a roughly 1,000-acre site, with Phase 1 including two ship berths and a turning basin, and Texas LNG is progressing a second export terminal. Marine construction of that scale means barges, tugs, spuds, moorings and new structures — and inspection work that is about verifying installed condition rather than assessing decay. See [marine construction dive support](/services/marine-construction-support/).

**The shrimp fleet.** The Brownsville–Port Isabel fleet runs roughly 140 boats and is second in the nation by value and volume of wild-caught shrimp, and the largest and most valuable commercial fishery in Texas. Trawlers foul their running gear on their own gear, on debris and on each other's lost nets. That is high-frequency, fast-turnaround work — usually a [propeller inspection and clearing](/services/propeller-services/) job that starts as an inspection and becomes a recovery.

## What you receive

An inspection is only worth what its documentation is worth. A verbal "looks fine" from someone who has just climbed out of the water is not an inspection, and it will not survive contact with a surveyor, an insurer or a buyer's lawyer.

A report should contain, at minimum:

- **What was examined**, item by item, against the vessel's or structure's own references — frame numbers, compartment identifiers, berth and pile numbering — so that a finding can be relocated by someone who was not there.
- **What condition each item was in**, described rather than graded. "Coating breakdown with light surface corrosion over approximately two square metres, port side, frames 40–46" tells a reader something. "Fair" does not.
- **What was not examined, and why.** This is the part most often left out and the part that matters most later. If visibility, marine growth, an isolation that could not be obtained, or an inaccessible space prevented examination of something, the report says so. A report that implies complete coverage it did not achieve is worse than no report.
- **Still photographs and video**, with enough context in frame to establish location, and enough scale to establish size.
- **Where relevant, a recommendation** — and a clear line between what we observed and what we are advising.

If the inspection was triggered by an event, the report should separate observed damage from pre-existing condition wherever the evidence allows, and should say when it does not allow.

## Inspection, survey, or UWILD — which do you actually need?

These three get used interchangeably and they are not the same thing.

| | Who sets the scope | What it is for | Who reviews it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Condition inspection** | You | Your decision — maintenance, purchase, repair, sailing | You, your insurer, a buyer |
| **Damage survey** | You and usually your insurer | Establishing what an event did | Insurer, surveyor, sometimes counsel |
| **UWILD / in-water survey** | The class society | Credit in place of a drydocking | The attending class surveyor |

The practical route for most owners is the first, then the third. A condition inspection tells you whether the underwater body is in a state that a class surveyor is likely to accept in the water — coatings sound, anodes present and working, no damage requiring drydock repair. If it is not, you have found that out cheaply, before booking a survey that would have been abandoned.

For an ABS in-water survey specifically, the requirements are firm and worth knowing before you shop for a contractor. The examination covers the entire vessel below the waterline. The diver must be employed by a firm ABS has approved as a service supplier. The work is carried out on closed-circuit television with two-way communication, monitored by the attending Surveyor and/or documented photographically, depending on the age and type of vessel. And the water has to be clear enough for that single-frame propeller and rudder criterion.

> **Ask this before you book anyone.** Which class society, which survey, and does the contractor hold that society's service supplier approval for it? A contractor who cannot answer precisely is a contractor whose report may not be creditable — and you will find that out after the dive rather than before it.

## The standards any contractor should be held to

Commercial diving in the United States is regulated work, and the standard is not a matter of company preference. Under **29 CFR 1910 Subpart T**, surface-supplied air diving requires a minimum three-person dive team — a designated person-in-charge, the diver, and a tender — and the diver must be **continuously tended** while in the water. Surface-supplied air diving is limited to 190 fsw, with dives of 30 minutes bottom time or less permitted to 220 fsw. A decompression chamber must be ready at the dive location for any dive outside no-decompression limits or deeper than 100 fsw.

Those are the federal minimums, and they apply to everyone. Before any contractor enters the water on your vessel or your berth, it is entirely reasonable to ask for the written dive plan, the emergency and evacuation arrangements, confirmation of the isolation and lock-out of propulsion, thrusters and sea suctions, and the qualifications of the supervisor running the dive.

Isolation is the one worth insisting on. The most serious risk in in-water inspection is not depth or visibility — it is a machine starting while a diver is next to it. That is agreed with the vessel and the terminal before mobilisation and confirmed physically, not over the radio.

## Season, weather and when to book

Inspection work on this coast has a rhythm, and planning around it costs nothing while ignoring it costs schedule.

Atlantic hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November. Practically, that produces two distinct waves of inspection work. Before a system is forecast, owners and terminals want mooring arrangements, ground tackle and berth structures checked, and anything loose on the bottom identified. After one passes, the questions change: what moved, what is now on the seabed near the berth, whether a vessel that rode it out took contact, and whether the fenders and pilings that absorbed the surge are still sound. That second wave is diagnostic and time-critical, and it competes with everybody else on the coast asking the same questions in the same week.

The quieter part of the year is when planned work belongs. If a survey is due, a coating assessment is wanted, or a docking decision is coming, scheduling it outside the season means conditions are more predictable and the dive is less likely to be displaced by somebody's emergency — including your own.

Rainfall matters more than people expect. Freshwater coming down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande carries sediment into the system, and visibility in the channel drops for a period afterwards. For hands-on work that changes very little. For a survey with a visibility acceptance criterion, it can be the difference between a creditable survey and a wasted mobilisation, which is the argument for booking class work with some flexibility in the date rather than fixing it to a single morning.

## When an inspection is not the right answer

Occasionally the honest answer is that diving is not what you need.

If the vessel already has a confirmed defect that can only be repaired in a drydock, an inspection to characterise it further is often money spent to reach a conclusion you have. If the water is genuinely unworkable and there is no scheduling window that fixes it, the responsible answer is to say so rather than to dive and produce a report that documents very little. And if you are asking a question that a hull condition record, a coating history or the last drydock report would answer, that is cheaper than a dive spread.
