Underwater welding is the most misunderstood service in commercial diving, and the misunderstanding costs owners money. The phrase gets used as though it describes one thing with one level of quality. It does not. It describes a family of methods producing welds of deliberately different classes, and the entire commercial question is which class your repair actually requires.

This page explains the classes, the methods, what wet welding can and cannot achieve, and how to specify a repair so that what you get back is what you needed. It also says plainly when the answer is a drydock.

## The classes are the whole conversation

The governing document in the United States is **AWS D3.6M, the Underwater Welding Code**, currently the 2017 edition. It does something genuinely useful: it classifies welds by what they have to survive, rather than by how they were made.

| Class | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Class A** | Intended for applications and design stresses **comparable to their conventional above-water counterparts** |
| **Class B** | Intended for **less critical** applications, where limited discontinuities can be tolerated |
| **Class O** | Must conform to **another designated code or standard**, with additional requirements to cope with the underwater environment |

Read those definitions carefully, because the difference between them is the difference between a repair that carries load and a repair that holds something in place.

A Class B weld is not a bad weld. It is a weld made to a standard that explicitly accepts limited discontinuities, because the application does not need better. Using one where a Class A weld is required is the failure. So is paying for Class A where Class B was always sufficient.

**Class O is the one owners most often actually need without knowing the name.** If the item is classed, coded, or governed by some other specification, the weld has to conform to that specification — and D3.6M's Class O exists precisely to say so.

> **The question to ask any contractor.** Not "can you weld underwater" but **"which class, qualified to which procedure, and who accepts it?"** Almost nobody in this market puts that in writing. A contractor who answers it precisely is telling you something real about how they work.

### How quality is actually established

D3.6M sets, for each class, both the weldment properties that must be **established during qualification** and the weld soundness requirements that must be **verified during construction**.

That is the mechanism, and it is worth understanding because it is what separates a controlled repair from a hopeful one:

1. **The procedure is qualified in advance** — proven to produce welds meeting the class's requirements under representative conditions.
2. **The welder-diver is qualified to that procedure**, not generically.
3. **The completed weld is verified** against the soundness requirements for that class.

None of that happens spontaneously at the berth. It is arranged before mobilisation, which is why a genuine coded repair has a lead time and a repair sold as immediate usually is not one.

## The five methods, and why wet welding is what you will usually get

D3.6M covers five basic methods:

- **Dry welding at one atmosphere** — the weld site is enclosed and pressurised to surface conditions
- **Dry welding in a habitat** — a large enclosure the welder works inside
- **Dry chamber welding** — a smaller enclosure around the joint
- **Dry-spot welding** — a small local gas-filled space at the arc
- **Wet welding at ambient pressure** — the welder-diver is in the water, with **no mechanical barrier between the water and the welding arc**

The dry methods produce better and more consistent results because the arc is not being quenched by surrounding water. They also cost substantially more: the enclosure has to be engineered, built, positioned and sealed against the structure.

**Wet welding is what nearly all in-water repair on this coast actually involves.** It is a real, long-established technique, and it is the reason a repair can happen alongside at a berth this week rather than in a yard next quarter. Its limits are physical: rapid quenching affects the metallurgy of the weld and the heat-affected zone, hydrogen is a live concern, and the results are more variable than the same joint made in air.

The correct way to think about the trade is not "wet welding is worse." It is: **wet welding is a different process with a different achievable class, and the job is to match the class to the requirement.**

## What underwater welding is genuinely good at

On the South Texas coast, the work divides into a few recognisable patterns.

**Attachments and non-structural fittings.** Padeyes, brackets, doubler plates, anode mounting pads, guards and supports. This is bread-and-butter Class B work and wet welding does it well.

**Temporary and holding repairs.** Getting a vessel from where it is to a docking it can be repaired in properly. A patch over a defect, a doubler over a wasted area, securing something that has come adrift. Legitimate, common, and the right answer far more often than owners expect — provided everyone is clear that is what it is.

**Sealing and closure work.** Blanking plates over openings, cofferdam and closure work supporting other repairs. This overlaps heavily with [sea chest inspection and repair support](/services/sea-chest-services/), where overboard blanking is routinely the enabling step for work happening inside.

**Structural repair to a specified class.** Where the item and the conditions support it, and where the procedure and qualifications are in place beforehand.

**Cutting.** Frequently the larger half of the work: cropping out damaged steel before a repair, removing wreckage, freeing seized or fouled fittings, cutting away failed structure. Cutting arrives on the same jobs as welding, and on [marine salvage](/services/marine-salvage/) work it often arrives on its own.

## What it is not good at

Being clear about this is the most useful thing on the page.

**Full plate renewal.** If the wastage is extensive, a weld repair is treating a symptom. The steel needs replacing and that generally means a dock.

**Anything requiring a dry, prepared, controlled surface** to a standard wet conditions cannot deliver.

**Repairs where the consequence of failure is severe and the conditions will not support the required class.** If the item needs Class A or a coded repair and the location, access or water will not allow it, the honest answer is that the repair belongs elsewhere.

**Papering over a diagnosis that has not been made.** A crack is a symptom. Welding it without understanding why it appeared often means welding it again. Where the cause is unclear, [underwater inspection](/services/underwater-inspection/) comes first.

## How the Brownsville channel affects the work

Two local factors shape in-water welding here specifically.

**Visibility.** The channel carries silt, and freshwater coming down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande after rain drops clarity further. For welding this matters less than people assume — much of the work is done close in, by feel, with the welder-diver's hands on the joint. It matters more for the inspection either side of the weld, where the condition of the surrounding steel has to be established.

**The vessel and structure mix.** The port moved 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% in five years, and it is the only deep-water port directly on the US–Mexico border. Three local sectors generate distinct welding and cutting work:

- **Ship recycling and layberth.** The port captures more than 85% of US Navy and MARAD ship-recycling business, with SteelCoast alone working an 80-acre complex and roughly 4,000 ft of main-channel frontage. Inactive and awaiting-tow tonnage produces closure work, blanking, hull integrity repairs and a great deal of cutting.
- **LNG and marine construction.** Rio Grande LNG is building toward 48 MTPA with two ship berths and a turning basin in its first phase, and Texas LNG is progressing a second terminal. New marine structures mean attachment work, guards, supports and repair to installed steel — see [marine construction dive support](/services/marine-construction-support/).
- **The shrimp fleet.** Around 140 boats, second in the nation by value and volume of wild-caught shrimp. Smaller-scale but steady: guards, brackets, running-gear-related repairs and cutting away fouled gear.

## Specifying a repair so you get what you need

The difference between a good in-water repair and an expensive disappointment is almost entirely in how it was specified. Five things settle it.

**1. What is the item, and what does it carry?** Structural, pressure-retaining, watertight, or simply held in place. This determines the class before anything else is discussed.

**2. Who has to accept the repair?** Class society, Coast Guard, an insurer, a charterer, or only you. If it is a classed item, involve the society before work starts. Retrospective approval is a much harder conversation.

**3. Is it permanent or a bridge to a docking?** Both are valid. Confusing them is not. If it is a bridge, agree what "until" means — next scheduled docking, a specific date, or a defined operating limit.

**4. What is the condition of the surrounding steel?** Welding to wasted plate transfers the problem rather than solving it. This is why an inspection usually precedes a repair.

**5. What access and isolation are available?** Whether the joint can be reached, worked and inspected, and what has to be shut down and locked out while somebody is beside it with an arc.

Answer those five and a contractor can tell you honestly what is achievable. Skip them and you will get a price for something nobody has defined.

## Cutting, in a little more detail

Cutting gets treated as the junior partner to welding and on many jobs it is the larger and more urgent half of the work.

**Removal before repair.** Damaged or wasted steel usually has to come out before anything can go back in. Cropping out a section cleanly, to a line that the replacement can be fitted to, is the step that determines how good the subsequent repair can be.

**Clearing obstructions.** Steel that should not be where it is — a failed fitting, a section of collapsed structure, debris fouling a berth face or a grating. Often the entire job.

**Freeing what is seized.** Fittings, fasteners and components that corrosion has made immovable, where cutting is the only route left that does not damage what surrounds them.

**Salvage and recovery.** On a [marine salvage](/services/marine-salvage/) job, cutting is frequently how access is created, how lifting points are established, and how a wreck is reduced to pieces that can be recovered.

The planning considerations differ from welding in one important way: cutting is irreversible, and what is on the other side of the plate matters enormously. Establishing what a compartment contains, whether it is under pressure, whether it holds residues or a flammable atmosphere, and what structure is load-bearing, all has to happen before a torch is lit. That assessment is usually the reason a cutting job takes longer to plan than to perform.

## What a repair record should contain

An in-water repair without a record is a repair somebody will question later — a surveyor, an insurer, a buyer, or your own engineer trying to work out why the same defect has reappeared.

At minimum it should identify **what was repaired and where**, against the vessel or structure's own references, so the location can be found again. It should state **what class the weld was made to and against what procedure**, because that is the only meaningful statement about the repair's capability. It should record **who performed it and their qualification to that procedure**. It should describe **the condition of the surrounding material** — welding to sound plate and welding to wasted plate produce different futures. And it should say **what verification was carried out** against the soundness requirements for that class.

Where the repair is temporary, the record should say so unambiguously, and say what it is temporary until. That single line prevents the most expensive misunderstanding in this field: a holding repair inherited by somebody who was not there and assumed it was permanent.

## Safety, and what to ask before anyone gets in the water

Underwater welding and cutting combines two hazardous activities, and the standard is not a matter of company preference. Under **29 CFR 1910 Subpart T**, surface-supplied air diving requires a minimum three-person dive team — a designated person-in-charge, the diver, and a tender — with the diver continuously tended while in the water. Surface-supplied air diving is limited to 190 fsw, with dives of 30 minutes bottom time or less permitted to 220 fsw, and a decompression chamber must be ready at the dive location for any dive outside no-decompression limits or deeper than 100 fsw.

On top of the diving requirements, welding and cutting underwater bring their own: electrical safety and the control of the welding circuit, and the accumulation of hydrogen and oxygen in any enclosed or partially enclosed space near the arc. That second one is why enclosed-space work adjacent to hot work is planned rather than improvised.

Before any contractor starts, ask for the written dive plan for that specific job, the supervisor's qualifications, the emergency arrangements, confirmation of how propulsion, thrusters, sea suctions and the electrical system will be isolated and locked out, and — for anything structural — the welding procedure and the class it is qualified to.

Isolation deserves particular insistence on welding work. The diver is at the plate, often near openings and machinery, holding a live electrode.

## Cost, and why the cheap quote is usually the expensive one

Owners comparing in-water welding quotes are often comparing genuinely different products described in the same words, and the gap is rarely the day rate.

**Team size.** A compliant surface-supplied spread is three people minimum. A quote that only works with two has left out the tender, and the tender is not an optional refinement — the diver has to be continuously tended.

**Whether a qualified procedure exists.** A repair made to a qualified procedure by a welder qualified to it, and verified afterwards, costs more than an unqualified weld, because that work is real and takes time. If one quote is markedly lower, establish whether it includes any of it.

**Inspection either side of the weld.** Establishing the condition of the surrounding steel before, and verifying the weld after, is what makes the repair a known quantity. Skipping both is cheaper and leaves you with something nobody has assessed.

**Mobilisation.** A crew travelling from the Houston corridor is roughly three hundred miles of road for the people and the spread, and that lands in the price whether or not it appears as a line. It also lands in the schedule, because a crew already on this coast can take a berth window that a travelling crew cannot.

The useful comparison is not the number at the bottom. It is: what class is the weld, is there a qualified procedure behind it, who is verifying it, and what happens if the inspection finds the surrounding plate is not sound enough to weld to.
