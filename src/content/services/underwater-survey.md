Most underwater work asks about a built thing — a hull, a pile, a berth. This service asks about everything else: the ground, what is on it, what is in it, and what conditions anyone working there is going to meet.

It is the least glamorous item on our list and it is disproportionately the one that prevents expensive surprises, because almost every marine project makes assumptions about the bed and most of those assumptions come from a drawing.

## What a condition survey covers

**Seabed composition and profile.** What the bottom is made of, how it varies across the area, and what the levels are against a stated datum.

**What is on the bed.** Debris, dropped material, abandoned structure, old moorings, and whatever else has accumulated. In a port with a long working history this is rarely nothing.

**What is in the bed**, to the extent it can be established. Probing establishes depth to firm material and locates near-surface buried objects.

**Scour and deposition patterns**, particularly around structures. See [pile, dock and pier inspection](/services/pile-dock-pier-inspection/), with which this frequently runs together.

**Route corridors.** Cable and pipeline routes: position against the as-laid record, depth of cover, exposure, free spans and any damage or interference.

**Intake and outfall approaches**, where the surrounding bed determines whether a structure keeps working.

**General working conditions.** Current, visibility, access and hazards, for anyone about to plan a job there.

## Why the drawing is not the answer

Marine projects run on records, and underwater records age badly.

**As-laid is not as-designed.** What was actually installed frequently differs from what was drawn, and the difference is documented inconsistently.

**The bed moves.** Deposition and scour change profiles continuously. A survey from five years ago describes a bed that no longer exists in detail.

**Things accumulate.** Working ports collect material on the bottom, and none of it is on any drawing.

**Previous work leaves things behind.** Cut-off piles, abandoned moorings, temporary works that were never fully recovered, and structure from facilities that no longer exist. This port has been working long enough to have accumulated a good deal of it.

**Services move or become exposed.** A cable laid with adequate cover can be exposed years later by scour, and its recorded position may not be where it is.

The practical consequence: **the survey is what converts a drawing into a fact**, and it is almost always cheaper than the discovery it prevents.

## Surveying water you cannot see through

The Brownsville Ship Channel carries silt, and rainfall through the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande reduces clarity further. Bed survey here is largely done by method rather than by eye, and the method is what makes it reliable.

**Grid and transect work.** The area is divided against fixed references and covered systematically, so that coverage is a known quantity rather than an impression.

**Probing.** A rod pushed into the bed establishes the depth to firm material and detects buried objects near the surface. This is how a soft, deep silt layer is distinguished from a thin veneer over firm ground — a distinction that matters enormously to anyone planning to put a spud, an anchor or a pile there.

**Tactile identification.** Objects are identified by feel, dimension and where possible by local excavation.

**Measurement against a datum.** Levels are meaningless without a stated reference, and a survey that does not say what its levels are relative to cannot be compared with anything.

Where the area is large, acoustic methods are the sensible first pass — covering ground quickly and identifying where divers should look. That sequence, rather than a choice between the two, is usually the efficient answer. What divers add is identification, probing, measurement and confirmation, none of which a sonar record provides on its own.

## Pre-dredge and post-dredge survey

Dredging is where this work has the clearest return.

**Before.** Establishing the starting profile, and — more importantly — finding what is in the way. A dredger that encounters an obstruction may be damaged by it, and a damaged cutter head costs vastly more than the survey that would have found the obstruction. Old piles, abandoned structure and substantial debris are exactly what turns up in a port with this history, particularly given the scale of current development: Rio Grande LNG building toward 48 MTPA with two ship berths and a turning basin, and Texas LNG progressing a second terminal.

**After.** Confirming the completed profile against specification, particularly at edges, slopes and around structures where plant cannot reach cleanly. This is what the payment usually depends on.

**Alongside existing structures.** Excavating near a berth changes the ground supporting it. Monitoring bed levels around structures during and after dredging is how you establish whether anything has been undermined — and finding that at the time is considerably better than finding it at the next structural inspection. See [marine construction dive support](/services/marine-construction-support/).

## Route surveys

Cables, pipelines and services crossing or running along the bed need periodic confirmation, and the findings that matter are usually the same three.

**Position against record.** Where it actually is, compared with where the as-laid says it is. Discrepancies matter for anyone about to excavate, anchor or drive nearby.

**Depth of cover.** How much material is over it now, which is not what it was when it was laid.

**Exposure and free spans.** The findings with real consequences. An exposed service is one anchor, one spud or one dropped object away from a serious incident. A free span — where the bed has been removed beneath a length of it — introduces stresses it was not designed for, and can develop into a failure without anything ever touching it.

Where a service is found exposed, the useful report says the extent, the levels, and the condition of the service where it is exposed, so whoever owns it can decide between monitoring, reburial or protection.

## Working condition surveys

Sometimes the question is simply: what is it like down there?

Somebody is planning work — a repair, an installation, a lift, a mooring — and needs to know what conditions the working party will meet. Current at different tide states, realistic visibility, the nature of the bed and whether it will support plant, access, and what hazards are present.

This is a small, cheap survey that routinely changes a method statement, and it is frequently skipped in favour of assumptions drawn from a chart. The chart does not tell you that the bed at a particular berth is soft to two metres, or that the current runs harder than expected between two structures, or that there is a wire across the area that nobody has recorded.

## Anchorages and approach areas

Not all survey work is at a berth. Anchorages, turning areas, approach channels and lay-up areas all have the same characteristics — they are used on the assumption that the bed is as recorded, and that assumption is rarely tested.

**Anchorage holding.** Whether the bed will hold an anchor is a function of what it is made of and how deep the workable material runs. A charted anchorage with a thin layer of soft material over hard ground behaves very differently from one with deep, uniform holding, and the difference matters most in exactly the conditions where holding is being relied on.

**What is already down there.** Anchorages accumulate. Lost anchors, chain, wire and debris are common, and an anchor that fouls on something is both a recovery problem and a potential damage claim. In a port with substantial layberth and recycling activity, and decades of use, the accumulation is real.

**Turning basins and approaches.** Where vessels manoeuvre under power, the bed takes the energy. Deposition and scour patterns in these areas are frequently different from the adjacent channel and less often surveyed.

**Lay-up areas.** Where inactive tonnage sits for long periods, both the bed condition and what has been dropped on it over the years are worth establishing before anything is moved.

These areas tend to be surveyed after an incident rather than before one, which is the wrong way round for the same reason it is everywhere else in this trade: the survey that prevents the incident costs a fraction of the one that investigates it.

## Seasonal and event-driven surveys

Atlantic hurricane season runs 1 June to 30 November, and storm surge rearranges bottoms. After a system, the useful questions are what moved, what is now on the bed near berths and structures, whether channels and approaches are still clear, and whether anything previously buried is now exposed.

That last one deserves attention. A storm that removes a metre of cover from a cable or pipeline has created a hazard that did not exist before and will not be visible to anybody until somebody looks. Post-storm survey is one of the highest-value pieces of work available in this window, and demand for it is concentrated across the whole coast in the same few days.

## What the report contains

**The area covered**, defined precisely, with the method used and the reference framework.

**Bed composition and profile** against a stated datum.

**Objects located**, with position, dimensions, and identification where possible — and an honest description where identification was not possible.

**Probing results**: where, to what depth, and what was found.

**Conditions encountered** — current, visibility, and anything bearing on future work.

**What was not examined and why.** Areas not covered, depths not probed, objects not identified. This is what makes the rest of the report usable, because it tells a reader precisely how far the findings extend.

Where the survey will be compared against a previous one, the referencing and datum have to match. Where it is the first, it becomes the baseline, and it is worth taking the time to establish that framework properly because everything afterwards is measured against it.

## Why the bed matters structurally

It is worth spelling out the connection between a bed survey and structural condition, because the two are usually commissioned separately and are not separate problems.

Almost every marine structure derives part of its capacity from the ground. Piles get lateral support and, through embedment, a substantial share of their load capacity from the material around them. Gravity structures rely on the bearing capacity of what they sit on. Moored systems depend entirely on what holds their anchors.

**Change the bed and you have changed the structure**, even though nobody touched it. A pile in perfect metallurgical condition with two metres of scour around its base is not the pile that was designed. A quay wall whose toe has been undermined is carrying its load differently than intended. An anchor whose surrounding material has been dredged away holds less.

This is why bed levels belong in a structural inspection scope rather than in a separate survey commissioned later, and why the two are worth doing together. It is also why any excavation near existing infrastructure warrants monitoring rather than assumption.

The relevance here is immediate. The June 2026 channel deepening took the main channel from 42 ft to 52 ft and the entrance and jetty channels to 54 ft, and substantial construction continues around the port. Both the deeper channel and the larger vessels it brings change the energy reaching beds around existing structures. A facility whose bed profile was last established before those changes is working from a picture that has moved.

## Establishing a baseline worth having

A first survey becomes the reference for everything afterwards, and a few decisions at that point determine how useful it will be for the next decade.

**Fix the datum and record it explicitly.** Levels relative to an unstated reference cannot be compared with anything. This sounds obvious and is the most common reason two surveys of the same area cannot be reconciled.

**Establish physical reference points** that will still be findable in five years — structural features, marked positions, or recorded coordinates. A survey referenced to something temporary is a survey that cannot be repeated.

**Record the method and the coverage**, so a later survey can be run the same way. Comparing a systematic diver survey against a sonar pass is comparing two different things.

**Record conditions at the time.** Tide state, recent weather and recent rainfall all affect what a survey finds, particularly in a channel where sediment load varies with rainfall. A finding that looks like deposition may be a difference in conditions between two surveys.

Getting these right costs almost nothing at the time and is essentially impossible to retrofit.



Survey work has a particular hazard profile because the diver is working in an area whose contents are, by definition, not yet known.

**Unknown objects.** Anything not identified is treated as potentially sharp, unstable, under load or hazardous.

**Entrapment**, among debris and structure, in water where a developing problem cannot be seen. Line management and tending discipline are the controls, and in poor visibility the tender's line is frequently the diver's only reference.

**Current**, which varies across a survey area and around structures.

**Traffic**, where the survey area is in or near a working channel carrying 28 million tons of cargo a year.

**Buried services**, which is a reason probing is done with care and with whatever records exist to hand rather than blind.

Commercial diving in the United States operates under 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, setting the minimum dive team, continuous tending, depth limits and decompression provisions applying to every contractor equally. On survey work the discipline that matters most is the systematic one — a survey worked carelessly is not merely less useful, it produces a record somebody will later rely on.

## What a survey cannot tell you

Being clear about the limits is what makes the findings usable, and it is the section most survey reports omit.

**Full buried profiles.** Probing establishes depth to firm material at the points probed and can find objects near the surface of the bed. It does not map what is deep or continuous between probe points. A report claiming a complete buried picture from a diver survey is claiming more than the method supports.

**Material properties.** A survey establishes what the bed feels like and how far a probe goes into it. It does not establish bearing capacity, shear strength or anything else a geotechnical investigation provides. Where those numbers matter — and for anything being founded on the bed they do — the survey informs where to investigate rather than replacing the investigation.

**Condition inside a service.** A route survey establishes where a cable or pipeline is, what cover it has and whether it is exposed or damaged externally. Its internal condition is a separate question with separate methods.

**What will happen next.** A single survey is a snapshot. It shows deposition or scour as a state, not as a rate. Only a repeat survey against the same datum turns it into a trend, and the trend is usually the commercially useful part.

**Anything outside the covered area.** This sounds trivial and is the most frequently misread part of any survey. An area not covered is not an area confirmed clear, and reports should be written so that distinction cannot be missed by somebody reading quickly.

We would rather set those limits out at the quotation stage than have a report relied on for something it cannot support. Where the question genuinely needs a method we do not provide, we will say which method it needs.

## Commissioning a survey

Tell us the area, the question you actually need answered, and what records exist. The question matters more than the area: "confirm the bed is clear for a jack-up" and "establish the profile for a dredging specification" and "find out whether our outfall is still buried" all produce different surveys of the same water.

Send whatever you have — charts, as-laid drawings, previous surveys, dredging records. The survey is usually cheaper and better when it starts from what is already known and sets out to confirm or correct it, rather than starting from nothing.

We will come back with the method we would use, the coverage that produces, what we can and cannot establish about anything buried, and what the report will contain. Where an acoustic first pass would make the job faster and cheaper, we will say so rather than selling diver hours to cover ground a sonar could cover in an afternoon.
