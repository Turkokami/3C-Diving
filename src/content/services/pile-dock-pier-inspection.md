Marine infrastructure fails invisibly. A quay, a pier or a berthing structure spends its life with the most vulnerable part of itself under water, and the part above water can look entirely sound while the part below is losing section, losing support, or both. By the time a problem is visible from the deck, it is usually well past the point where it could have been dealt with cheaply.

This page covers what actually degrades marine structures, what an underwater inspection establishes, why scour matters more than most owners expect, and what makes a report usable by the engineer who has to act on it.

## What we inspect

**Piles**, of every common material — steel tubular and H-section, reinforced concrete, and timber — through the tidal and splash zones down to and below the mudline.

**Sheet pile walls and bulkheads**, including the interlocks, any visible tie-back arrangement, and the condition of the wall face.

**Deck structure from below.** Beams, pile caps, connections, bracing, and the underside of the deck where it is accessible.

**Fender and berthing systems.** Fender units, their fixings, chains and mountings, and the structure behind them.

**Dolphins, mooring points and their structural connections.**

**The seabed around and beneath the structure** — which is where scour lives, and which is frequently the most important part of the whole inspection.

**Intakes, outfalls and services** passing through or beneath the structure, which overlaps with [seabed and underwater condition survey](/services/underwater-survey/).

## What degrades a marine structure

The mechanisms differ by material, and knowing which one you are looking at determines what matters.

### Steel

**Corrosion, concentrated by zone.** Steel in seawater does not corrode uniformly with depth. The splash and tidal zones typically suffer worst, because they combine oxygen availability with wetting and drying. Below the permanent waterline the rate generally falls. That zoning is why an inspection has to cover the full height rather than sampling at a convenient depth, and why the worst section loss is often just above where most people stop looking.

**Accelerated low water corrosion** is a recognised phenomenon in some marine environments, producing unexpectedly rapid loss in a narrow band near low water. Where it is present it can be severe and localised enough to be missed entirely by an inspection that does not go looking for it.

**Coating breakdown**, which precedes and accelerates everything above.

**Weld and connection condition**, particularly at splices, bracing connections and anywhere the structure changes section.

**Impact damage**, which on a berthing structure is routine rather than exceptional.

### Concrete

**Cracking**, with the pattern being diagnostic — the orientation and distribution say something about the cause.

**Spalling**, where the surface has come away, frequently driven by corroding reinforcement beneath expanding as it corrodes.

**Exposed and corroding reinforcement**, which is both a symptom and an accelerant.

**Chloride attack**, the underlying mechanism in seawater, working inward until it reaches the steel.

**Impact and abrasion damage**, particularly at berthing faces.

### Timber

**Marine borer attack**, the defining problem. Organisms that consume timber from within can leave a pile looking sound externally while the interior is largely gone. This is the classic case where visual inspection alone is misleading and probing matters.

**Abrasion, splitting and mechanical damage**, plus the condition of bolts, straps and connections, which frequently fail before the timber does.

## Scour: the failure nobody sees coming

Of everything on this page, scour is the one most often underestimated.

A pile derives lateral capacity from the ground around it and from its embedded depth. **Scour removes that ground.** Current, propeller wash from vessels manoeuvring, and the disturbance of berthing all mobilise seabed material and carry it away from around the structure.

The consequences are structural rather than cosmetic. Less embedment means less capacity. Less surrounding material means less lateral restraint and a longer unsupported length, which changes the structure's behaviour under load in ways the original design did not contemplate. And because the pile itself may be in perfect condition, a visual inspection focused on the pile can pass a structure that has lost a significant part of what was holding it up.

**Propeller wash is a major driver in a working port.** Modern vessels have substantial installed power, and thruster use during berthing directs energy straight at the bed near the structure. A berth that has seen a change in the size or type of vessel calling — which is exactly what happens when a channel is deepened — may be experiencing scour conditions it was never designed for.

That is directly relevant here. The Brazos Island Harbor deepening completed in June 2026 took the main channel from 42 ft to 52 ft and the entrance and jetty channels from 44 ft to 54 ft, making Brownsville the third deepest port in Texas. Deeper channels bring deeper-draught, generally larger and more powerful vessels. **Any structure whose scour history predates that change is worth reassessing against what is calling now**, and a bathymetric picture of the bed around a berth is the way to establish it.

Measuring scour means establishing bed levels around the structure and, critically, comparing them against previous surveys or against the design. A single survey tells you the current state. A repeated one tells you the rate, and the rate is what determines whether this is a monitoring item or an intervention.

## What an inspection produces

The output has to be usable by somebody who was not there — normally a structural engineer, an asset manager, or whoever is deciding on a maintenance budget.

**Findings located against the structure's own references.** Pile numbers, bay references, chainage, elevation relative to a stated datum. A finding nobody can relocate is a finding nobody can act on.

**Description rather than grading.** "Section loss over approximately 300 mm of the circumference, 1.2 m below mean low water on pile 14, with pitting to an estimated depth of 6 mm" is actionable. "Fair condition" is not. Where a grading system is required — and some asset owners have one — it is applied on top of the description, never instead of it.

**Dimensions where dimensions matter.** Crack widths and lengths, extent of spalling, measured section loss, scour depths against a datum.

**Imagery with scale and context**, so a reader can see both what it is and how big it is.

**Bed levels around the structure**, where scour is in scope.

**An explicit statement of what was not examined and why.** Areas obscured by growth that was not removed, sections below the mudline, spaces that could not be accessed, or areas where visibility prevented proper examination. This is the section that makes the rest of the report credible.

**What we do not do is issue a structural assessment.** We report condition. Whether the structure is fit for purpose, what capacity it retains and what should be done are engineering judgements that belong to your engineer, and a diving contractor claiming them is overreaching.

## Marine growth is part of the job

You cannot inspect what you cannot see or touch. On a structure that has not been attended to in years, growth may completely obscure the surface being assessed.

So cleaning is frequently a precursor, and it needs to be in the scope and the price from the start. The usual approach is to clean representative areas thoroughly rather than the entire structure — enough to establish condition reliably, targeted at the zones where problems concentrate and at any area where something has been found.

An inspection quoted without any allowance for this is either assuming a clean structure or planning to report on what can be seen through the growth. Both are worth asking about.

## Conditions on the Brownsville Ship Channel

**Visibility.** The channel carries silt, and rainfall down the Arroyo Colorado and the Rio Grande reduces clarity further. A substantial proportion of structural inspection here is done close-in and by touch, with the camera near the surface being examined. Experienced divers assess corrosion, section loss, cracking and borer damage tactilely with considerable reliability. What poor visibility genuinely constrains is wide-angle context imagery, which is worth knowing if the report is going to a reader who wants to see the whole bay in one frame.

**Current and traffic.** Working the face of an operational berth means working around vessel movements and in whatever current the tide is producing. This is a scheduling input rather than an obstacle, and it is one of the practical advantages of a crew that works this water regularly.

**The structures themselves.** This is a port in the middle of substantial development. Rio Grande LNG is building toward 48 MTPA including two ship berths and a turning basin, Texas LNG is progressing a second terminal, and the port handled 28 million tons of cargo in FY2025, up 101% over five years. That produces both new structures needing baseline and construction-phase inspection, and older structures now serving traffic they were not designed around.

## Baseline inspections on new structures

New construction is the cheapest possible time to inspect, and it is frequently skipped because everything is obviously fine.

The value is not in finding defects. It is in establishing a **baseline**: what the structure looked like, what the bed levels around it were, and what was actually built, recorded at the moment nobody disputes any of it.

Every subsequent inspection is then a comparison rather than a fresh assessment, and comparison is enormously more informative. Scour is a rate rather than a depth. Section loss is a trend rather than a number. Damage can be attributed to a period rather than argued about. Structures without a baseline spend their whole lives being assessed in absolute terms, which is much harder and much less useful.

This applies to construction-phase inspection too — verifying that what is under water matches what was specified, while the contractor is still on site and it can be dealt with. See [marine construction dive support](/services/marine-construction-support/).

## Who the report is really for

A structural inspection report has several readers with different needs, and understanding that changes how it should be written.

**The asset manager** wants to know what is deteriorating, how fast, and what it will cost to do something about it. They are planning budgets across a portfolio and they need condition expressed consistently enough to compare one structure against another and this year against last.

**The structural engineer** wants observations, not conclusions. Dimensions, locations, extents and the evidence behind them, so they can form their own judgement about capacity and remaining life. A report that leaps to "the pile is adequate" has done their job badly and taken away the information they needed to do it properly.

**The operations team** wants to know whether anything affects how the berth can be used tomorrow — load restrictions, vessel size limits, or areas to keep clear.

**Insurers and, occasionally, lawyers** want a contemporaneous record of condition at a date, made by somebody competent, that distinguishes clearly between what was observed and what was inferred.

Those needs are compatible, but only if the report is built from observation upward. A description with dimensions and location serves all four. A grading serves only the first, and only if everybody agrees what the grades mean.

This is why we push back gently on requests for a simple pass or fail. Where an asset owner has a formal grading system we will report into it, and we will put the underlying description alongside it — because the grade is a summary and the description is the evidence, and in three years' time when somebody is comparing against a new survey it is the description that carries the information.

## After an event

Routine inspection is on a cycle. Event-driven inspection is not, and knowing what constitutes an event is worth having agreed in advance.

**A hard berthing.** Even where the fender did its job, the energy went somewhere.

**A storm.** Atlantic hurricane season runs 1 June to 30 November, and surge moves seabed material, loads structures in unusual directions and puts debris against them. Post-storm inspection is one of the most valuable things an asset owner does, and demand for it is concentrated into the same week across the whole coast.

**A grounding or incident nearby**, which can affect the bed around a structure even if nothing touched it.

**A change in the vessels calling**, as above.

**Anything unexpected in the structure's behaviour** — movement, a change in how it feels underfoot, a fender behaving differently, a crack appearing above water.

## Repair follows inspection, and the sequence matters

An inspection that finds something usually leads to a repair conversation, and the order in which that happens determines how well it goes.

**Establish extent before method.** The commonest error is to price a repair from the first observation. Corrosion found at one pile is a data point; whether it is a local defect or the visible edge of a condition affecting a whole row is a completely different question with a completely different budget. Extending the inspection to establish extent is cheap relative to specifying the wrong repair.

**Understand the mechanism.** A repair that addresses the symptom without the cause buys time and no more. Scour that is refilled without addressing the flow condition returns. Corrosion patched without restoring protection continues beside the patch. Reinforcement covered without dealing with chloride contamination keeps corroding underneath.

**Decide what can be done in the water.** Some repairs genuinely can: pile jackets and wraps, localised concrete repair systems, [anode installation](/services/anode-replacement/) to restore or improve cathodic protection, and structural attachment work where the class and condition support it. Others need the structure out of service or need work from above, and there is no benefit in discovering that after mobilising.

**Get the baseline before the repair.** The condition record made immediately before an intervention is what any future assessment of that repair's performance is measured against. It is a small addition to work already being done and it is routinely forgotten.

Where a repair is carried out, the same documentation standard applies as to the inspection: what was done, where, to what specification, and what condition the surrounding structure was in when it was done.

## Safety on structural work

Berth and pile work has a different hazard profile from vessel work.

**Vessel movements** are the main one. A vessel manoeuvring at or near the berth while divers are in the water alongside it is unacceptable, and controlling that requires the terminal's active cooperation and a clear communication protocol — not just a note in a plan.

**Entrapment** among structure, bracing, debris and accumulated material, made considerably worse by poor visibility.

**Current**, particularly around and between piles where flow accelerates.

**Overhead work and dropped objects**, where the berth is operational above.

Commercial diving in the United States is governed by 29 CFR 1910 Subpart T, setting the minimum dive team, continuous tending, depth limits and decompression provisions applying to every contractor. For structural work, the additional job-specific controls around vessel movements and berth access are usually where the real planning effort goes.
